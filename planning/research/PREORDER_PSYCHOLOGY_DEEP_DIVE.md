## Executive Summary (12 headline conclusions)

1. **Pre-orders are not just early purchases; they are ownership-claiming acts.** The Carlson School work (Kim & Hedgcock, University of Minnesota, working paper for JCP) shows across five experiments (total N ≈ 2,295) that consumers pre-order *even without tangible benefits* because pre-ordering satisfies a "claim motivation" — the desire to lock in future ownership of a product they already feel psychological ownership over. Eliciting psychological ownership raised reservation prices by **14%** in a pre-order context but not in a regular-order context. For anticipy.ai this means the single highest-leverage copy strategy is to make visitors *feel the pendant is already theirs* — "Claim yours," "Reserve your unit," self-product imagery prompts — rather than leading with discounts.

2. **Psychological ownership is manufacturable before legal ownership.** Touch (Peck & Shu 2009), ownership imagery ("imagine you have taken it home"), haptic imagery, length of exposure, and self-product elaboration all reliably raise psychological ownership, which in turn raises valuation (endowment-like effects of roughly +20–65% in valuation across paradigms). A pre-order page cannot offer touch, but it can offer its proven substitutes: ownership imagery, configurators, engraving/customization, 3D interactive models.

3. **The classic endowment effect is large (WTA/WTP ≈ 2:1) and survives — indeed grows — with temporal delay on the buying side being the weak link.** Delaying transactions reduces willingness to pay more than willingness to accept, which *widens* the WTA–WTP gap for future transactions. Implication: buyers discount future products steeply, so the page must actively compress psychological distance to August 2026 (Section 2) and add anticipation value (Section 8).

4. **Psychological distance is the pre-order's core conversion tax, and construal fit is the remedy.** Construal level theory (Trope & Liberman 2010) implies a product shipping in ~12 months is represented abstractly ("an AI pendant") rather than concretely ("what will I actually do with it on Tuesday morning"). Promotions and copy that match construal level to release timing work better: abstract "why" appeals + desirability framing far from release; concrete "how" appeals + feasibility detail near release. A pre-order page live for a year should *stage* its messaging accordingly.

5. **Mental simulation is the cheapest proven intervention.** Process simulation (imagining using the product) and outcome simulation (imagining the benefits) reliably increase purchase intention for new products; Castaño et al. (2008, JMR) show which simulation type works at which temporal distance (visualization/outcome simulation in the distant future; process simulation and testimonials near launch). Ownership imagery is the pre-order-specific version.

6. **In crowdfunding persuasion, both central (argument-quality) and peripheral (cue) routes matter, and group-based cues dominate for low-elaboration backers.** Allison et al. (2017, JBV; 383 Kickstarter ventures + a 150-participant conjoint experiment; companion 2015 ETP study of ~185k Kiva loans) show issue-relevant information (experience, ingredient branding b = 2.41, product interconnections b = 3.05) and peripheral cues (backer counts, group identity language) jointly drive funding; low-elaboration contexts amplify peripheral cues. anticipy.ai's page must serve both routes simultaneously: spec-and-credential blocks for elaborators, social-proof counters and identity language for skimmers.

7. **Backer motivation is a portfolio: product acquisition first, but helping/identity/community are load-bearing.** Gerber & Hui (2013) and multiple systematic reviews converge: reward-based backers are motivated by (i) getting the product early/exclusively, (ii) supporting creators and causes they identify with, (iii) belonging to a community of early adopters, and (iv) fun/engagement in the process. Deterrents: distrust of delivery and creator capability. A pre-order page that behaves like a "campaign" (updates, founder visibility, backer counts) captures motives (ii)–(iv) that a plain checkout leaves on the table.

8. **Advance selling is profitable because of buyer-side uncertainty and seller-side commitment, but the psychology of paying is asymmetric: deposits feel cheap now and painful later.** Xie & Shugan (2001) formalize when advance selling adds profit; mental-accounting work (Prelec & Loewenstein 1998; Nan et al. 2023) shows prepayment "decouples" payment pain from consumption pleasure — full payment now converts August delivery into something that feels *free* on arrival, while deposit-balance schemes concentrate pain at the worst moment (just before shipping). Recommendation: full payment (or payment plan completed well before ship date), never a large balance due at ship.

9. **How you *say* August 2026 matters.** Sokolova (2022, JCR; 8 preregistered studies, N = 4,758) shows day-of-week/date framing changes perceived duration; dates ("ships August 2026") are processed as calendar events and feel closer than durations ("ships in 12 months"). Related work on date/delay framing (LeBoeuf 2006; Malkoc & Zauberman 2006) confirms: **always frame the wait as a date, never as a number of days/months**, and anchor on the *event* ("in time for [use occasion]").

10. **Waiting can be an asset: anticipation carries positive utility, especially framed experientially.** Loewenstein (1987) shows people will pay *more* for a delayed kiss than an immediate one — anticipation ("savoring") has hedonic value; Kumar, Killingsworth & Gilovich (2014) show waiting for experiential purchases is more pleasurable (less impatient, more positive) than waiting for material ones. anticipy.ai should frame the pendant as an experience-enabler (your AI companion, captured memories) and program the wait (updates, milestones, early app access) to convert dead time into savoring.

11. **Lenient refund policies increase purchases more than they increase returns.** Janakiraman, Syrdal & Freling (2016, J. Retailing) meta-analysis (21 papers, 11,662 subjects) finds return-policy leniency significantly increases purchase (with money- and effort-leniency most potent for purchase) while effects on actual returns are smaller and dimension-dependent. For a 12-month wait, a generous, prominently displayed "cancel anytime before shipping for a full refund" policy is close to free conversion — it removes the dominant perceived risk (Section 6 deterrents) at modest cost.

12. **The post-purchase period is a retention battlefield, not an afterthought.** Cognitive dissonance is highest for expensive, deliberation-heavy, irreversible purchases with long delivery gaps; Kickstarter data (Mollick 2014) shows delays are endemic (over 75% of delivered projects ship late) and delay severity scales with project size. Dissonance-reduction levers: immediate post-purchase reassurance, steady progress updates, community, and — counterintuitively — the same refundability that drove conversion. A silent 12 months between checkout and shipping will produce cancellations that a $2/month email cadence would have prevented.

# SECTION 1 — Psychological Ownership and the Endowment Effect in Pre-Orders

## 1.0 Why this literature is the backbone of pre-order psychology

A pre-order is a purchase in which payment precedes possession, sometimes by many months. The naive economic view says this is strictly worse for the consumer than buying at release — money is surrendered earlier, consumption is not accelerated, and risk (non-delivery, product disappointment, better alternatives appearing) is absorbed. Yet pre-ordering is a mass behavior, including for digital goods with infinite inventory and no pre-order discount. The literature reviewed in this section explains the paradox: consumers can *feel ownership* long before they *have* the object, feelings of ownership raise valuation (the endowment effect), and the act of pre-ordering itself satisfies a motivation to *claim* an object one already feels is "mine." The pre-order page is therefore not primarily a price-and-logistics instrument; it is an ownership-feeling induction instrument.

The section proceeds: theory of psychological ownership (1.1), the classic endowment effect (1.2–1.3), ownership without legal ownership (1.4–1.7), the Carlson School pre-order work in depth (1.8), imagery-based substitutes for touch (1.9–1.10), the endowment effect under temporal delay (1.11–1.12), self-investment/co-creation effects (1.13–1.14), and a synthesis with page-level applications (1.15).

## 1.1 Pierce, Kostova & Dirks (2001; 2003) — the theory of psychological ownership

**Citation:** Pierce, J. L., Kostova, T., & Dirks, K. T. (2001). Toward a theory of psychological ownership in organizations. *Academy of Management Review*, 26(2), 298–310; and Pierce, J. L., Kostova, T., & Dirks, K. T. (2003). The state of psychological ownership: Integrating and extending a century of research. *Review of General Psychology*, 7(1), 84–107. (Access: abstract + extensive treatment in the full texts of Peck & Shu 2009, Shu & Peck 2011, and Kim & Hedgcock, which build directly on this framework.)

**Sample/method:** Conceptual/theoretical papers (no sample). They define psychological ownership as the state in which an individual feels that a target (material or immaterial) is "MINE," distinct from legal ownership, and identify three *routes* to psychological ownership: (1) **control** over the target, (2) **intimate knowledge** of the target, and (3) **self-investment** (investing time, energy, effort, self into the target). Its underlying motives are efficacy/effectance, self-identity, and "having a place" (home).

**Effect sizes:** N/A (theory). The three routes have since been operationalized and confirmed experimentally throughout consumer research (see 1.4–1.10).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The three routes are a literal design checklist for a pre-order page for a product no one can touch for a year. (1) *Control:* let visitors configure the pendant — chain length, finish, engraving, name their AI, choose its voice — before checkout; interactive 3D models the visitor can rotate/zoom simulate physical control. (2) *Intimate knowledge:* progressive disclosure of "insider" detail (design teardown, titanium machining process, firmware roadmap) makes early buyers feel they know the pendant the way an owner knows their possession. (3) *Self-investment:* asking pre-order customers to invest effort — join a naming vote, complete a "set up your AI's memory profile" onboarding before the device ships — converts waiting into self-investment that deepens ownership. Because the pendant is an AI whose value comes from *personalization*, anticipy.ai has an unusually strong self-investment channel: the customer's AI can begin learning about them from day one via the app, months before hardware arrives, making the eventual physical pendant feel like it was always theirs.

## 1.2 Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990) — the canonical endowment effect

**Citation:** Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1990). Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem. *Journal of Political Economy*, 98(6), 1325–1348. (Access: abstract via Semantic Scholar + parameter values replicated and reported in the full text of Shu & Peck 2011 and Reb & Connolly 2007, both read in full.)

**Sample/method:** Series of incentive-compatible market experiments with Cornell and Simon Fraser undergraduates. Participants randomly endowed with a good (coffee mugs ~$6 retail, pens) either sold (WTA), bought (WTP), or chose between good and cash ("choosers," who face the seller's wealth position without ownership). Real transactions were executed.

**Effect sizes:** The signature result is a WTA/WTP ratio around **2:1** — e.g., median selling prices for mugs roughly $7 vs. median buying prices roughly $3; choosers valued mugs near buyers, not sellers, showing the gap is caused by *ownership*, not wealth position. Under-trading: where Coase predicts ~50% of mugs should trade, observed trading volume was roughly half that. The effect persisted across repeated markets (not eliminated by learning).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The endowment effect defines the asymmetry a pre-order page must engineer around: *prospective buyers systematically undervalue what they don't yet own by roughly half relative to owners*. Two implications. First, the $149.99 price will be judged by "buyer psychology" — the page's job is to shift visitors toward "owner psychology" before the price is evaluated, using the ownership-induction tools in 1.4–1.10 (imagery, customization, possessive language: "your pendant," "your AI"). Second, once someone *has* pre-ordered, the endowment effect flips to the seller's favor: cancellation now feels like *losing* "my pendant," not like foregoing a purchase. This is why cancellations during a 12-month wait are rarer than economics predicts, and why post-purchase ownership reinforcement (serial number assigned at purchase, "Unit #4,182 reserved for you," the AI already active in the app) makes refund-risk from Section 9's generous cancellation policy manageable: you are arming loss aversion in favor of retention.

## 1.3 Morewedge & Giblin (2015) — what actually drives the endowment effect

**Citation:** Morewedge, C. K., & Giblin, C. E. (2015). Explanations of the endowment effect: An integrative review. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 19(6), 339–348. (Access: abstract + publisher summary; full-text excerpts via the Cell Press page.)

**Sample/method:** Integrative review of the experimental literature. Contrasts loss-aversion accounts with "psychological ownership" accounts, organizing evidence around attribute sampling biases (owners preferentially attend to positive attributes), reference prices, and ownership-self associations.

**Effect sizes:** Review; reports the field's range of WTA/WTP disparities (commonly ~2:1, up to 14:1 for scarce experience goods such as NCAA Final Four tickets, citing Carmon & Ariely 2000).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Two mechanisms in this review are directly exploitable. (1) *Biased attribute sampling:* owners value objects more because ownership directs attention to positive features. A pre-order page can induce owner-like attention by asking visitors to *generate* positive-use thoughts (see query theory, 1.5) — e.g., an interactive "What would you ask your pendant first?" prompt forces value-increasing queries before price consideration. (2) *Self-object association:* the more the product is linked to the self, the bigger the effect — engraving initials, the AI addressing the visitor by name in a demo, or a quiz ("what should your AI remember about you?") builds the self-link. The 14:1 ratio for scarce experiential goods also hints at the ceiling: if the pendant is framed as a scarce, experience-generating good (founding-member batch, numbered units), attachment and retention should be far stronger than for a commodity gadget.

## 1.4 Peck & Shu (2009) — mere touch and ownership imagery create ownership without ownership (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Peck, J., & Shu, S. B. (2009). The effect of mere touch on perceived ownership. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 36(3), 434–447. (Access: full text via Internet Archive Scholar copy of the JCR PDF.)

**Sample/method:** Study 1: N = 231 buyers (non-owners), 2 (touch: touch vs. no touch) × 2 (ownership imagery: present vs. absent) between-subjects, with two haptically different objects (a Slinky toy and a coffee mug); DVs: perceived ownership (Pierce-based scale, α ≈ .96–.97) and valuation. Study 2 extends to sellers (owners) and adds pleasant vs. unpleasant touch.

**Effect sizes (Study 1):** Main effect of touch on perceived ownership F(1, 227) = 23.58, p < .001; main effect of ownership imagery on perceived ownership F(1, 227) = 5.80, p = .02 and on valuation F(1, 227) = 7.94, p = .005 (valuation means, e.g., $3.36 vs. $2.75 — a ~+22% lift from imagery alone). Touch raised valuation when touch was pleasant; unpleasant touch *lowered* valuation (Study 2), i.e., the mechanism is affect-laden, not mechanical.

**Key sentence for pre-orders:** ownership imagery — "imagine taking the product home with you. Where would you keep it?" — increased both perceived ownership and valuation *in the absence of any touch*, i.e., ownership feelings are inducible purely mentally.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** This is the license to run "imagery = touch" on a page where physical touch is impossible until August 2026. Concretely: (a) an imagery block written in second person and future-perfect ("It's on your collar as you leave the house. You ask it to remember where you parked...") placed *above* the price; (b) an interactive 360° render the user drags — self-directed motion is the closest digital analogue of touch, and haptic-imagery research (1.9) shows imagined touch substitutes for real touch; (c) lifestyle photography engineered for "haptic richness": macro shots of the brushed titanium texture, the clasp action, the weight in an open palm — cues that let the visitor simulate the feel; (d) an explicit "Where will you wear yours?" selector (collar / chain / pocket clip) that forces the ownership-imagery question in Peck & Shu's own format. The warning in their Study 2 also matters: imagery of *unpleasant* sensations backfires — so avoid any copy that triggers negative haptic simulation (weight complaints, skin irritation debates) anywhere near the conversion path, and preempt them in FAQ language framed positively ("lighter than a house key: 11 g").

## 1.5 Shu & Peck (2011) — psychological ownership + affective reaction mediate the endowment effect across nine studies (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Shu, S. B., & Peck, J. (2011). Psychological ownership and affective reaction: Emotional attachment process variables and the endowment effect. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 21(4), 439–452. (Access: full text, author-hosted UCLA Anderson PDF.)

**Sample/method:** Nine studies replicating canonical endowment paradigms (BDM auctions with real pens worth ~$1.50; binary-choice paradigms) with added measurement of psychological ownership (3-item scale) and affective reaction (18-item PANAS-based), then mediation tests. Undergraduates, N ≈ 30–71 per study.

**Effect sizes (selected, from the full text):**
- Study 1 (baseline): sellers' valuation $2.30 vs. choosers' $1.67 (F(1,65) = 5.36, p = .02) — a **+38% ownership premium**; psychological ownership 4.69 vs. 2.28 (F = 45.99, p < .001). Both psychological ownership (Sobel z = 4.58) and positive affect (z = 2.21) mediated; neither alone sufficed — full mediation only jointly.
- Study 2 (length of ownership; replicating Strahilevitz & Loewenstein 1998): 30 minutes of possession vs. minutes raised selling price $1.71 → $2.34 (**+37%**; F(1,69) = 5.52, p = .02) and psychological ownership 3.97 → 4.87 (p = .005) with *no* change in affect — duration works purely through ownership feelings.
- Study 3 (query theory; choosers only, no endowment at all): merely listing value-increasing reasons *first* raised valuation $1.44 → $1.89 (**+31%**; F(1,56) = 4.26, p = .04) and psychological ownership 2.17 → 2.91 (p = .07).
- Study 4 (focus on the forgone) and Studies 5–9 replicate market-experience, emotion (disgust/sadness), and unpleasant-touch moderators; e.g., sadness raised choosers' valuations to $2.71 (higher than sellers'), disgust suppressed the effect.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Three page mechanics fall straight out of the mediation evidence. (1) *Time-on-target is ownership* (Study 2): design for dwell — the longer a visitor interacts with "their" pendant (configurator, demo conversation with the AI, scrolling an interactive story), the more psychological ownership accrues; a "save your configuration" email capture extends possession across sessions, so the returning visitor greets *their* pendant, not *a* pendant. (2) *Query order is a conversion lever* (Study 3): put prompts that elicit value-increasing thoughts before any consideration of cost — e.g., "Pick the 3 things you'd want your AI to handle first" as an interactive step ahead of the buy box; never open with objection-handling ("Why $149.99?") because that queues value-decreasing thoughts first. (3) *Both cognition and affect are needed* (Study 1): specs alone (ownership cognition) or mood alone (pretty film) underperform their combination — pair the emotional film with an immediately-following personalization step. Finally, the emotion moderators are a caution for retargeting: ads reaching users in incidental negative states interact unpredictably with valuation; anchor retargeting creative in warm, efficacious moments (morning routines, travel) rather than FOMO-anxiety.

## 1.6 Reb & Connolly (2007) — possession, not legal ownership, drives the endowment effect (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Reb, J., & Connolly, T. (2007). Possession, feelings of ownership and the endowment effect. *Judgment and Decision Making*, 2(2), 107–114. (Access: full text, Cambridge open PDF.)

**Sample/method:** 2 (factual ownership: yes/no) × 2 (physical possession: yes/no) between-subjects with a chocolate bar; incentive-compatible valuation (choice list between the bar and rising cash amounts from $0.10), plus measured subjective ownership ("I feel like I own the chocolate bar, even if I don't legally own it").

**Effect sizes:** Significant main effect of **possession** on valuation, F(1, 47) = 4.83, p < .05, ω² = .07; in the full factorial, only possession (not factual ownership) significantly affected feelings of ownership, F(1, 95) = 5.10, p < .05, ω² ≈ .04. Factual ownership without possession produced *no* significant valuation premium.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** This is the cleanest evidence that the psychological machinery of "mine" runs on *proximal experience*, not paperwork — which cuts both ways for a pre-order. The bad news: an order confirmation email (legal ownership without possession) generates surprisingly little endowment by itself; a buyer who receives nothing but a receipt for 12 months is psychologically close to a non-owner and cancels easily. The good news: *possession-like experiences* can be delivered digitally long before the hardware ships. Give every pre-order customer immediate "possession" of something: activate their AI companion in the app the moment they order (it's *their* AI now — it has their name, their preferences, their conversation history); issue a numbered digital certificate; show "your pendant" with their engraving rendered in their account dashboard. The strategic principle: **collapse the possession gap to zero on the software side even though the hardware gap is 12 months.** This converts the order from "legal ownership without possession" (weak endowment, per this paper) into "psychological possession now, hardware later" (strong endowment).

## 1.7 Strahilevitz & Loewenstein (1998) — duration of ownership compounds value

**Citation:** Strahilevitz, M. A., & Loewenstein, G. (1998). The effect of ownership history on the valuation of objects. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 25(3), 276–289. (Access: abstract + direct replication with full statistics in Shu & Peck 2011, Study 2, read in full.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments manipulating duration of ownership (and history of past ownership) of small objects before eliciting valuations; replicated by Shu & Peck (2011) with 71 undergraduates in the 30-minutes-vs-minutes design.

**Effect sizes:** In the Shu & Peck replication: valuation $1.71 → $2.34 (+37%) from ~30 minutes of additional possession; psychological ownership 3.97 → 4.87, fully mediating. Original paper shows valuation increases with duration of ownership and even with duration of *past* (lost) ownership.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Duration effects imply the 12-month pre-order window can be an *asset* for attachment rather than only a churn risk: every month a customer spends with their app-active AI, their configured pendant page, and their member community is a month of accruing ownership history — by August 2026 the shipped pendant arrives into a relationship that is a year old. Operationally: (a) start the "ownership clock" visibly at purchase ("You've been a member since September 2025"; "Your AI has remembered 214 things for you so far"); (b) for visitors who don't buy, even short pre-purchase possession matters — a 7-day free trial of the companion app before committing hardware money exploits duration-of-ownership on the trial side (with the endowment effect then working against ending the trial); (c) the "past ownership" finding (losing something once owned raises its valuation) is the psychological engine behind cart-abandonment emails that show the abandoned configuration as something being *given up* — "Your pendant configuration expires in 48 hours" is a loss frame that this literature licenses (use honestly).

## 1.8 KIM & HEDGCOCK (Carlson School, University of Minnesota) — "Purchase Now, Own Later": the claim-motivation account of pre-ordering (FULL TEXT READ — centerpiece)

**Citation:** Kim, S., & Hedgcock, W. (2025, working paper / dissertation, in preparation for *Journal of Consumer Psychology*). Purchase now, own later: The desire to claim ownership explains preordering without benefits. Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. (Access: full text, Carlson-hosted PDF, 105 KB, read in full including all five studies and supplementary notes.)

**Core theory:** Pre-orders impose an inherent temporal delay between payment and consumption. For consumers who already feel psychological ownership of an unreleased product, this gap creates a **claim motivation**: a desire to lay a claim on the product — to convert uncertain future ownership into certain future ownership — that pre-ordering uniquely satisfies (a regular purchase transfers ownership immediately, so there is nothing to "claim"). Hypotheses: (H1) satisfying claim motivation increases pre-order intent; (H2) psychological ownership fuels claim motivation; (H3) the psychological-ownership → purchase-intent link is *stronger* in pre-order than regular-order contexts.

**Studies, samples, and statistics (all from the full text):**
- **Study 1** (N = 500 Prolific, 29.2% female, prescreened video-game hobbyists; GTA 6 pre-order): a claim-framed promotion ("be among the first to claim...") vs. a $2 discount. Claim promotion beat the financial promotion on pre-order intent for consumers with **high psychological ownership** of the game/franchise: b = 0.62, t = 2.77, p = .006 (no effect at low psychological ownership). Notably, a small discount was *not* superior — money was the weaker motivator.
- **Study 2** (N = 600 Prolific, 53.5% female, Mage 39.8): manipulated psychological ownership via **self-product elaboration** (imagine yourself using the game's features) vs. product elaboration (evaluate features). Self-product elaboration raised psychological ownership (M = 3.73 vs. 3.20, F(1,598) = 12.07, p = .001); claim (vs. discount) promotion again raised intent (p = .004). Psychological ownership → intent was stronger under claim promotions, but only for *low-expertise* consumers (b = 0.28, p = .015) — experts already had high baseline ownership (expertise correlated r = .65 with psychological ownership, r = .57 with intent).
- **Study 3** (N = 400 Prolific; Paris 2024 Olympics T-shirt; pre-registered logic on self vs. gift): elaboration × target interaction F(1,361) = 4.38, p = .037. Self-product elaboration raised pre-order intent for self-purchases (b = 0.47, p = .059) but not gifts (b = −0.28, ns) — because you cannot "claim" ownership of something that will be someone else's. Moderated mediation: elaboration → psychological ownership b = 0.84, p < .001; conditional effect of ownership on intent: self b = 0.63 vs. gift b = 0.38 (indirect effects 0.63 [.364,.924] vs. 0.32 [.159,.526]).
- **Study 4** (N = 400 Prolific; pre-registered; hypothetical new Tesla Model 3; pre-order with 6-month delivery vs. regular order with immediate delivery): self-product elaboration raised purchase intent overall (M = 3.57 vs. 3.06, F(1,398) = 6.41, p = .012), mediated by psychological ownership (elaboration → PO b = 1.66, p < .001; PO → intent b = 0.71, p < .001). Critically, index of moderated mediation significant (b = 0.28, CI [.034,.562]): PO → intent stronger for **pre-order (b = 0.79)** than regular order (b = 0.62); indirect effect 1.32 vs. 1.03.
- **Study 5** (N = 395 → 364 Prolific; pre-registered; Nintendo Switch game "Little Nightmares 3"; WTP-range methodology with minimum / need-consideration / maximum WTPs): elaboration × order-type on acceptable price range F(1,353) = 3.94, p = .048; three-way interaction with WTP index F(1,709.77) = 2.38, p = .018. Eliciting psychological ownership expanded the acceptable price range **only for pre-orders**, and the expansion was *upward*: maximum WTP (reservation price) rose from $32.2 to $41.1 in the pre-order condition — **+$7.8, i.e., +14%** (post-hoc d = 0.11, p = .028), with no expansion for regular orders.
- **Supplementary studies:** a deposit-style partial-payment framing had no significant effect on pre-order intent (Supplementary Study 1) — consistent with claiming (not financing) being the active mechanism.

**Assessment:** This is a working paper (strong design: 3 of 5 studies pre-registered, large Prolific samples, converging DVs including incentive-relevant WTP), not yet peer-reviewed — weight accordingly. But its two headline results triangulate with the rest of this section: ownership feelings are inducible by imagination alone, and their monetary consequences are *larger under pre-order conditions than normal retail conditions*.

**Application to anticipy.ai (extended, because this is the single most on-point source in the review):**
(a) **Lead with claiming, not saving.** The strongest promotion for high-ownership visitors was claim-framed, and even a discount did not beat it. anticipy.ai's primary CTA should be "**Claim your pendant**" / "**Reserve unit #___**" rather than "Pre-order and save." If a launch discount exists, subordinate it visually to the claim frame.
(b) **Manufacture psychological ownership with self-product elaboration immediately before the CTA.** Kim & Hedgcock's manipulation was literally "imagine how it fits into your life, what you'd feel toward it, then write it down." Page version: a 20-second interaction — "Tell your pendant one thing to remember about you" with a live AI reply — is a self-product elaboration exercise disguised as a demo, and per Study 4 it should raise intent by roughly half a scale point through ownership.
(c) **Segment by expertise/awareness.** Ownership-induction works hardest on *low-familiarity* visitors (Study 2); AI-enthusiast early adopters arrive pre-endowed. Cold traffic (Instagram ads) should land on elaboration-heavy pages; warm traffic (newsletter, waitlist) can go straight to claim-framed checkout with unit numbers and batch scarcity.
(d) **Do not optimize the page for gift purchases** during the pre-order phase — the entire ownership channel shuts off for gifts (Study 3). If gifting matters (holidays 2025), use a *different* mechanic: gift cards that let the recipient claim/configure their own pendant, restoring the recipient's claim motivation.
(e) **Price confidence:** Study 5 implies that induced psychological ownership raises the *reservation price* by ~14% specifically in pre-order framing. At $149.99, that is roughly the difference between "need to think about it" and "acceptable" for a marginal segment — i.e., ownership-induction is worth approximately a $20 price cut, at near-zero marginal cost.
(f) **Certainty is the product being sold.** The theoretical driver is converting *uncertain* future ownership into *certain* future ownership. Every uncertainty cue (vague ship dates, "subject to availability," reports of preorder oversubscription without guarantees) feeds claim motivation only if claiming *resolves* it. So pair scarcity ("Founding batch: 5,000 units") with an explicit certainty guarantee ("Pre-ordering guarantees your unit from the August 2026 production run") — scarcity creates the ownership threat; the pre-order must be framed as the *complete* remedy.

## 1.9 Peck, Barger & Webb (2013) — haptic imagery substitutes for actual touch

**Citation:** Peck, J., Barger, V. A., & Webb, A. (2013). In search of a surrogate for touch: The effect of haptic imagery on perceived ownership. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 23(2), 189–196. (Access: abstract + detailed treatment in the full text of the J-STAGE haptic-imagery papers below, which replicate and extend it.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments comparing actual touch, haptic imagery (imagining touching with eyes open), and no-touch controls on perceived ownership and valuation.

**Effect sizes:** Haptic imagery increased perceived ownership and valuation to levels comparable with actual touch ("imagining touching an object... increases perceived ownership in a manner similar to actual touch," as summarized in the replication literature); effects strengthened with imagery vividness.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Direct product-page instructions work: "Close your hand — that's about the weight. Run your thumb over brushed titanium." Video that shows a thumb tracing the pendant's surface, ASMR-adjacent clasp sounds, and slow-motion handling footage all scaffold haptic imagery for visitors who will not touch the product for a year. Since vividness moderates the effect, invest in *macro texture* photography over glamour renders: the aim is that the visitor's somatosensory imagination can find something to grip. This is cheap relative to its evidentiary support (three independent lines: Peck & Shu 2009; Peck et al. 2013; the J-STAGE replications in 1.10).

## 1.10 Kamleitner & Feuchtl (2015) and J-STAGE haptic-imagery replications (FULL TEXT READ for the latter)

**Citations:** Kamleitner, B., & Feuchtl, S. (2015). "As if it were mine": Imagery works by inducing psychological ownership. *Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice*, 23(2), 208–223 (access: abstract, summarized in the J-STAGE full text as "product imagery spontaneously elicited a sense of ownership that mediated the positive association between imagery and [purchase-related outcomes]"); and Sudo, M., et al. Mere touching imagery promotes purchase intention through increased psychological ownership. *Transactions of Japan Society of Kansei Engineering / SHES* 14(1), 49– (full text read via J-STAGE), plus companion papers on haptic imagery, effectance motivation, and psychological ownership (J-STAGE, full texts).

**Sample/method (J-STAGE lead paper):** Japanese adult samples; correlational + SEM designs relating haptic imagery, physical-control perceptions, familiarity, psychological ownership, valuation, and purchase intention across high- and low-haptic-importance products.

**Effect sizes:** SEM path structure supported imagery → perceived physical control → psychological ownership → purchase intention; positive correlations between imagery elaboration and ownership across product types (exact coefficients product-specific). Kamleitner & Feuchtl: imagery→ownership→preference mediation significant across studies.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The mediation chain (imagery → control → ownership → intent) says *control* is the middle link — imagery works best when it is imagery of *controlling* the product. For an AI pendant, the controllable moments are conversational: the page's imagery prompts should be command-shaped ("Ask it to..." / "Tell it to remember...") rather than passive ("It listens all day"). An embedded sandbox where the visitor actually issues three commands to a demo AI is the strongest possible "physical control" surrogate for a device with no buttons to press. Familiarity's role in the SEM also justifies content marketing that makes the pendant feel familiar before purchase (day-in-the-life videos in ordinary settings — commute, kitchen, school pickup — not sci-fi staging).

## 1.11 Buechel & Lehmann / JDM — the endowment effect in the future: delay hurts buyers more than sellers (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** (Authors as published) The endowment effect in the future: How time shapes buying and selling prices. *Judgment and Decision Making* (Cambridge open access; full text read). Experiment 1 N ≈ 300 (150 WTA, 149 WTP per the reported cells); Experiment 2 rules out alternative explanations.

**Sample/method:** Between-subjects WTA/WTP elicitations for a target good with transaction timing manipulated (today, weeks, 1 year). Medians compared across delay conditions with Mann-Whitney tests and regressions with clustered SEs.

**Effect sizes:** Baseline endowment effect: median WTA today $20.2 vs. WTP today $10.0 (z = 5.51, p < .01) — the classic ~2:1. Across delay: **WTA stayed roughly constant (even ticking up at 1 year: $21+), while WTP fell monotonically with delay** — so the WTA–WTP gap *widens* for future transactions. Regressions confirm delay significantly decreases WTP but not WTA.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** This quantifies the pre-order conversion tax: *willingness to pay for a delayed good sags with delay, mechanically*. A $149.99 pendant delivered today and the same pendant delivered in 12 months are different products in the buyer's value space — the August 2026 version is worth meaningfully less to them. Countermeasures, in order of evidence: (1) shrink *psychological* delay even when actual delay is fixed (date framing, Section 8; concrete near-future consumption of the app, 1.6); (2) deliver part of the product now (app access at purchase = partial immediate consumption, directly removing delay from part of the bundle); (3) load the offer with anticipation utility (Section 8's savoring literature) so the wait itself carries value; (4) price acknowledging the tax — early pre-order pricing below planned retail (e.g., $149.99 pre-order vs. $199 at release) is not merely promotional custom; it is compensation for delay-degraded WTP, and should be framed exactly that way ("early price for those willing to wait with us"). The seller-side result (WTA doesn't fall) again predicts strong retention *after* purchase: once endowed, customers' valuation won't decay just because shipping is far away.

## 1.12 Georgantzis & Navarro-Martinez / arXiv (2018) — assigned ownership alone elicits endowment (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** (arXiv:1809.08500) Eliciting the endowment effect under assigned ownership. (Access: full text, arXiv PDF.) Google Customer Survey, N = 495, randomized 4-group design; airline seat features (legroom; recline) priced via WTA or WTP.

**Sample/method:** Participants randomly told they either had a feature (and asked WTA to give it up) or lacked it (WTP to acquire), for features they plausibly encounter (5-hour flight recline/legroom). No physical possession at all — pure verbal assignment.

**Effect sizes:** WTA substantially exceeded WTP under mere verbal assignment (paper's core finding); response-pattern analyses (NA-rate asymmetries; compliance differences significant by t-test) consistent with genuine valuation asymmetry rather than survey artifact. (The paper is a working paper; treat as corroborating, not anchoring.)

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Mere *assignment language* moves valuations. On-page, this justifies "assignment micro-copy" long before checkout: once a visitor configures a pendant, the interface should speak as if the unit exists and is being held — "Your pendant (Space Titanium, 22-inch chain) is reserved in this browser for 48 hours." Waitlists work the same way: "You hold place #1,204" assigns a possession (the place) whose surrender (leaving the waitlist, or being passed over) engages loss aversion. The airline-features context also suggests applying assignment framing to *features*: "Founding members keep lifetime access to the Memory Vault" (assigned, WTA-framed) will defend attach-rate better than "Add Memory Vault for $X" (WTP-framed).

## 1.13 Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012) — the IKEA effect: labor creates love

**Citation:** Norton, M. I., Mochon, D., & Ariely, D. (2012). The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 22(3), 453–460. (Access: abstract + widely reproduced statistics.)

**Sample/method:** Four experiments; participants built IKEA boxes, folded origami, assembled Lego, then bid (incentive-compatible) on their own vs. others' creations.

**Effect sizes:** Builders bid ~63% more for their own assembled IKEA boxes than non-builders bid for identical pre-assembled ones (e.g., $0.78 vs. $0.48); origami makers valued their (amateur) creations near expert-made origami, while non-makers valued them near zero. Effect requires *completion* — incomplete labor destroyed the premium.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Self-investment (Pierce's third route) has a quantified consumer form: effortful co-creation raises valuation by half or more. The pre-order flow should include a *completable* act of labor: a 5-step "Build your AI" onboarding (choose voice → name it → teach it 3 facts → set its first daily briefing → preview it working) that ends in a completed, personalized artifact. The completion requirement is a sharp design constraint: never leave the customer's setup half-finished during the wait (progress bars stuck at 60% for months would actively *suppress* the IKEA premium); sequence the year of waiting as a series of small, individually completable projects (September: voice tuned ✓; October: memory profile complete ✓), each one banking additional self-investment into the pendant they will finally receive.

## 1.14 Fuchs, Prandelli & Schreier (2010) — empowerment-as-ownership in product creation

**Citation:** Fuchs, C., Prandelli, E., & Schreier, M. (2010). The psychological effects of empowerment strategies on consumers' product demand. *Journal of Marketing*, 74(1), 65–79. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Lab and field experiments in which consumers were "empowered to select" which products a firm should produce (vs. firm-selected identical products); DVs: WTP, purchase intention, loyalty.

**Effect sizes:** Consumers empowered to select the marketed products showed significantly higher demand — stronger purchase intentions, higher WTP (auction-measured), and more loyalty — toward the *identical* products, mediated by **psychological ownership of the outcome**; effects attenuate when outcomes diverge from the individual's own vote.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Give the pre-order community *real* selection power over pre-launch decisions with visible consequences: vote on the second finish color, the launch voice options, which integrations ship first, the name of the assistant's "remember" feature. Everyone who votes acquires partial psychological ownership of the *product line*, not just their unit — and Fuchs et al. show that translates into WTP and loyalty for identical goods. The moderator (effects fade when the outcome contradicts one's vote) dictates mechanics: use approval-style votes where most participants' preferences are partially satisfied ("all three colors won a production slot; order of release decided by vote") rather than winner-take-all polls that manufacture losers among your own pre-order base.

## 1.15 Section synthesis — the ownership stack for a pre-order page

Ranked by strength of evidence and proximity to the pre-order context:

1. **Claim-framed CTAs and certainty guarantees** (Kim & Hedgcock — 5 studies, 3 pre-registered, in-context): "Claim / Reserve," unit numbers, guaranteed-batch language. Expected impact: promotion-effectiveness ≈ exceeds a small discount; reservation price +14% when paired with ownership induction.
2. **Self-product elaboration / ownership imagery immediately pre-CTA** (Kim & Hedgcock S2–S5; Peck & Shu 2009; Kamleitner & Feuchtl): interactive "imagine/tell it" moments. Expected impact: +0.4–0.5 scale points intent; ~+20% valuation.
3. **Digital possession at purchase** (Reb & Connolly; Strahilevitz & Loewenstein): app-active AI, numbered certificate, ownership clock. Primarily a *retention* lever across the 12-month wait.
4. **Query-order control** (Shu & Peck S3): value-increasing prompts before price. ~+31% valuation in the lab analogue.
5. **Haptic-imagery assets** (Peck et al. 2013; J-STAGE): texture macro, handling video, command sandbox.
6. **Completable co-creation** (Norton et al.; Fuchs et al.): build-your-AI onboarding; community votes. Largest effects (+63%) but requires genuine effort mechanics.
7. **Assignment micro-copy** (arXiv; endowment-in-future JDM): reservation-hold language; feature-endowment framing; pre-order price framed as delay compensation.

**The single organizing sentence for anticipy.ai:** *the page's job is to make the visitor an owner before they are a buyer, and the wait's job is to make the owner more of an owner every month until August 2026.*

# SECTION 2 — Construal Level Theory and Psychological Distance for Future Products

## 2.0 Framing: a pre-order is a purchase at maximum psychological distance

Construal level theory (CLT) holds that the same object is mentally represented differently depending on its psychological distance from the perceiver — temporal, spatial, social, or hypothetical. Distant objects are construed abstractly (high level: purposes, desirability, "why") and near objects concretely (low level: means, feasibility, "how"). A product that ships in August 2026 sits far away on *two* distance dimensions at once: temporal (up to ~12 months) and hypothetical (it does not exist yet; specs could change; delivery is probabilistic). CLT therefore makes structured predictions about what pre-order visitors will care about, what messaging will fit, and how both should *change over the campaign's lifetime* as release approaches.

---

## 2.1 Trope & Liberman (2010) — the canonical statement of CLT (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Trope, Y., & Liberman, N. (2010). Construal-level theory of psychological distance. *Psychological Review*, 117(2), 440–463. (Access: full text via PubMed Central, PMC3152826.)

**Sample/method:** Theoretical synthesis over hundreds of experiments. Key mechanisms documented: (1) all four distance dimensions (temporal, spatial, social, hypotheticality) are cognitively interchangeable and mutually primed — IAT-style studies show people implicitly associate distance with high-level construal and proximity with low-level construal, with faster responses for congruent pairings; (2) distance shifts weighting from feasibility to desirability, from concrete features to abstract purposes; (3) predictions, evaluations, and choices all shift accordingly.

**Effect sizes:** Review paper; individual constituent studies typically show small-to-medium effects (see 2.2 meta-analysis for quantification).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Four concrete implications. (1) *Distances add up and cross-prime:* a page that emphasizes hypotheticality ("if we reach production...", crowdfunding-style uncertainty markers) makes August 2026 feel even further and the product more abstract — scrub conditional language; speak of the pendant as an existing object in production ("now in tooling; units ship August 2026"). (2) *Far-mode selling:* while the product is many months out, visitors weigh desirability (what life with an AI companion is like, identity, values — privacy-first, calm tech) over feasibility (setup steps, pairing, battery). Lead the 2025 page with the "why". (3) *Near-mode switch:* as August 2026 approaches, spontaneously re-weight the page toward feasibility — unboxing, setup-in-90-seconds, shipping logistics — because near-mode visitors penalize unresolved "how" questions. (4) *Social distance interacts:* testimonials from "people like me" (low social distance) concretize the product; celebrity/press endorsements are high-distance cues that fit the early abstract phase. Plan the page as a *construal gradient across the campaign calendar*, not a static artifact.

---

## 2.2 Söderberg, Callahan, Kochersberger, Amit & Ledgerwood (2015) — how big are CLT effects? (FULL TEXT READ, partial)

**Citation:** Söderberg, C. K., Callahan, S. P., Kochersberger, A. O., Amit, E., & Ledgerwood, A. (2015). The effects of psychological distance on abstraction: Two meta-analyses. *Psychological Bulletin*, 141(3), 525–548. (Access: full text PDF from author site; results tables partially extracted.)

**Sample/method:** Two meta-analyses over 106 papers / 267 experiments: (1) effects of distance on level of abstraction (125 studies with direct construal measures); (2) downstream consequences of distance-induced abstraction on evaluation, prediction, and behavior (426 effect sizes).

**Effect sizes:** A "reliable and medium-sized effect" of psychological distance on both abstraction and its downstream consequences (point estimates in the published tables are approximately Hedges' g ≈ 0.47–0.48 for both meta-analyses); effects replicate across time, researchers, and settings. Moderators: size of the temporal gap manipulated (bigger gaps → bigger effects) and DV sensitivity.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Two takeaways. First, CLT is not a fragile lab curiosity — medium, replicable effects justify building the campaign calendar around it. Second, the *gap-size moderator* matters enormously here: the difference between "ships next week" and "ships in 12 months" is at the top end of manipulated gaps in this literature, so anticipy.ai should expect construal effects near the larger end of the distribution — i.e., early-funnel visitors really will be in a qualitatively different evaluation mode than August-2026 visitors, and copy mismatched to that mode (feasibility detail in September 2025; abstract vision in July 2026) will measurably underperform. This licenses A/B testing infrastructure that varies message abstraction by countdown time — an unusual test dimension most teams never think to vary.

---

## 2.3 Liberman & Trope (1998) — desirability vs. feasibility over time

**Citation:** Liberman, N., & Trope, Y. (1998). The role of feasibility and desirability considerations in near and distant future decisions: A test of temporal construal theory. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 75(1), 5–18. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries; constituent results widely reproduced.)

**Sample/method:** Students chose between assignments/activities varying in desirability (value of the end state, e.g., interesting topic) and feasibility (ease of means, e.g., time required), for the near vs. distant future.

**Effect sizes:** Distant-future choices weighted desirability more and feasibility less; near-future choices reversed the weighting (interaction effects significant across four studies; the paradigm has been replicated extensively — e.g., people accept interesting-but-difficult tasks for "in 9 weeks" that they refuse for "this week").

**Application to anticipy.ai:** This is the sharpest single prediction for pre-order messaging: **in 2025, sell the ends; in summer 2026, sell the means.** Concretely, the pre-order page's hierarchy while shipping is ~a year away should be: (1) what the pendant makes possible (never forget a conversation, a second memory, presence without screens); (2) identity/values (titanium permanence, privacy-first architecture); (3) only then, mechanics. But the same literature warns of the *feasibility cliff*: when the distant becomes near (July–August 2026), buyers who committed on desirability suddenly re-evaluate on feasibility — battery life, app quality, comfort — and this is exactly when dissonance and cancellation risk spikes (Section 10). Pre-empt the cliff by drip-feeding feasibility proof *during* the wait (production videos, beta app reviews, real battery numbers), so the near-mode re-evaluation encounters answers rather than gaps.

---

## 2.4 Castaño, Sujan, Kacker & Sujan (2008) — what worries adopters of new products changes with temporal distance

**Citation:** Castaño, R., Sujan, M., Kacker, M., & Sujan, H. (2008). Managing consumer uncertainty in the adoption of new products: Temporal distance and mental simulation. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 45(3), 320–336. (Access: abstract + secondary descriptions; paywalled full text.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments on adoption intentions for innovations framed for near-future vs. distant-future adoption, measuring the *type* of uncertainties consumers generate and testing mental-simulation and other interventions matched to each.

**Effect sizes (as reported in abstract/secondary sources):** For distant-future adoption, consumers' dominant uncertainties are performance/symbolic outcome-related ("will it work? what will it say about me?"), and **outcome-focused simulations / visualization** reduce them and raise adoption intentions; for near-future adoption, uncertainties shift to switching-cost and usage/process concerns ("can I learn it? what's the hassle?"), and **process simulations** (imagining the steps of use) and concrete testimonials work better. Mismatched interventions underperform matched ones (significant interaction effects).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** This paper is the operational bridge between Section 2 and Section 3: it tells you *which mental simulation to deploy when*. Twelve months out, the page should run outcome simulation — vivid depictions of benefit states (a saved memory resurfacing at the right time; the "what did I promise Sarah?" moment answered) and symbolic reassurance (design press, who else wears one). As shipping approaches, rotate to process simulation: the 90-second pairing video, day-one setup walkthrough, "your first week with the pendant" email series for existing pre-order holders. Also apply it to *post-order* nurture — a September 2025 buyer will pass through both regimes during the wait, so the update cadence itself should migrate from outcome-flavored (vision, use stories) to process-flavored (setup, logistics) as August 2026 nears. This staged-uncertainty model also predicts *which FAQs to surface when*: symbolic/performance FAQs early ("does it actually remember everything?"), procedural FAQs late ("how do I pair it with my phone?").

## 2.5 Jha, Deitz, Hart & Royne Stafford (2019) — pre-order promotions must match construal to time-of-release

**Citation:** Jha, S., Deitz, G. D., Hart, P. M., & Royne Stafford, M. B. (2019). Sales promotions for preorder products: The role of time-of-release. *Psychology & Marketing*, 36(2), 113–124. (Access: abstract + conference-abstract PDF; paywalled full text.)

**Sample/method:** Series of experiments manipulating pre-order promotion type (price discount = concrete/low-level benefit vs. free gift/premium = can be framed abstractly) and time-of-release (soon vs. distant), measuring offer attractiveness and pre-order intentions.

**Effect sizes:** A pre-order promotion's effectiveness depends on whether it matches the construal level associated with the release timing (significant promotion-type × time-of-release interactions): concrete, transaction-level promotions (discounts, cash savings) fit near-release pre-orders; abstract or product-transcendent framings fit distant-release pre-orders. (Exact cell statistics unavailable — abstract-level access.)

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Direct guidance for promotion design across the 12-month window. While August 2026 is distant (i.e., through roughly Q1 2026), promotions should be high-level and end-state-linked: founding-member status, lifetime Memory Vault, engraved founders' batch, name-a-feature rights — benefits that live at the level of what the pendant *means*. Discount-led promotion in this phase is construal-mismatched (a concrete "save $20 today" collides with an abstract, distant product representation) and per Jha et al. will buy less conversion per dollar than the identical value delivered as an abstract benefit. In the final 8–10 weeks before shipping (and at general-availability launch), flip to concrete: "$149.99 → $199 on release day," free expedited shipping, bundled second chain. This also resolves an internal tension with Kim & Hedgcock (Section 1.8): their claim-framed (non-financial) promotion beating a discount was measured for *distant* releases — fully consistent with construal fit; expect discounts to regain traction only near release.

## 2.6 Zhou, Kimura & Doong (2024) — construal level in crowdfunding campaign text predicts performance

**Citation:** Zhou, L., Kimura, F., & Doong, H. (2024). Improvement of examining method with construal level theory perspective for reward-based crowdfunding performance. *Proc. IIAI International Conference on Advanced Applied Informatics*. (Access: abstract.)

**Sample/method:** Computational text analysis (LIWC-based linguistic markers of construal level) of Kickstarter-style campaign narratives, relating campaign-text construal level to funding performance.

**Effect sizes:** Reported as significant associations between narrative construal features and campaign outcomes (specific coefficients not available at abstract level); the contribution is methodological — construal level of *campaign copy* is measurable and performance-relevant.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Even if this specific paper is early-stage, it demonstrates a practical QA tool: score the pre-order page and every update email for linguistic concreteness (LIWC, or modern LLM-based concreteness raters) and *manage that score deliberately against the release countdown* per 2.3–2.5. In practice: set a target trajectory (abstract-skewed at launch, concreteness rising monthly), and audit drafted copy against it. This converts CLT from a vibe into a dashboard metric — e.g., "our July 2026 shipping-announcement email should score in the top concreteness quartile; our September 2025 brand film script in the bottom quartile."

## 2.7 Chen, Li & colleagues (2024) — innovation newness × ad construal fit

**Citation:** (Authors per journal record) Enhancing consumer evaluation of new products: The role of innovation newness and construal level of ad appeals. *Journal of Business Research* (2024), article S0148296324002704. (Access: abstract.)

**Sample/method:** Three studies crossing product newness (incrementally new products, INPs vs. radically new products, RNPs) with ad-appeal construal (abstract "why" vs. concrete "how"), measuring product evaluations.

**Effect sizes:** Significant fit effects: RNPs (which are psychologically distant by hypotheticality/unfamiliarity) evaluated better with construal-matched appeals; INPs with the opposite; moderated by consumers' processing orientation (exact statistics not available at abstract level).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** An AI pendant is a *radically* new product for most consumers — it lacks an established category schema ("is it a wearable? an assistant? a recorder?"). This work implies the newness itself adds psychological distance beyond time, and that abstract "why" appeals will fit cold audiences even *close* to release, because the category remains unfamiliar. Practical resolution of the tension with 2.3's "get concrete near release": concreteness should rise over time for the *usage/logistics* layer, while the *category-defining* layer ("a second memory, not another screen") stays stable and abstract throughout — anchoring the unfamiliar category to a familiar purpose. Also: comparative concreteness borrowed from adjacent familiar categories ("like AirPods for memory") is a known device for de-alienating RNPs; test one such analogy in the hero copy.

## 2.8 Malkoc & Zauberman (2006) — outcome concreteness changes delay sensitivity

**Citation:** Malkoc, S. A., & Zauberman, G. (2006). Deferring versus expediting consumption: The effect of outcome concreteness on sensitivity to time horizon. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(4), 618–627. (Access: abstract + secondary sources; 159+ citations.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments measuring how much compensation people require to *defer* consumption vs. how much they'll pay to *expedite* it, manipulating whether the outcome is represented concretely or abstractly.

**Effect sizes:** Deferral requires more compensation than expediting saves (deferral aversion); crucially, **concrete representations of the outcome amplify sensitivity to delay**, while abstract representations mute it (significant concreteness × time-horizon interactions).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** A subtle and important tension with everything above: making the pendant vividly concrete (Sections 1 and 3 say: do it — it builds ownership and simulation) *also* makes the 12-month wait more painful, because concrete outcomes make consumers more delay-sensitive. The synthesis: be concrete about *what the visitor gets now and during the wait* (app access today, founder community today, monthly progress) and *comparatively* abstract about hardware-in-hand moments until the ship date is close. Equivalently: never place your most vivid "it's in your hand" imagery directly beside the "ships August 2026" line — separate the maximum-vividness content from the maximum-delay information, and buffer the delay disclosure with the immediate deliverables. This is also an argument for the two-part product (software now, hardware later): it gives the concrete imagination something *proximal* to grip, discharging delay sensitivity harmlessly.

## 2.9 Section synthesis — the construal calendar for a 12-month pre-order

| Phase | Distance state | Dominant buyer questions | Message construal | Promotion type | Simulation type (Sec 3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Launch → ~T-9 months | Far temporal + high hypotheticality + category novelty | Why does this exist? Is it me? | Abstract: purpose, identity, values | Status/meaning perks (founders' batch, lifetime features) | Outcome simulation |
| T-9 → T-3 months | Middling; hypotheticality falling as production proof lands | Will it really work? Who's behind it? | Mixed; rising concreteness; proof artifacts | Hybrid (perk + modest financial) | Outcome → process transition |

Three standing rules: (1) never let hypotheticality cues (conditional verbs, "if funded," "estimated") survive editing — replace with production-certainty language; (2) manage concreteness as a measured property of copy (2.6); (3) keep vivid hardware imagery and delay disclosure apart (2.8).

# SECTION 3 — Mental Simulation Interventions

## 3.0 Framing: simulation is consumption's stand-in when the product can't be experienced

For a pre-order product, every ordinary source of pre-purchase experience — trial, touch, demo units in stores, friends who own one — is missing. Mental simulation (imagining using the product, or imagining the outcomes of using it) is the substitute channel through which value beliefs, ownership feelings (Section 1), and uncertainty reduction (Section 2.4) are built. The literature distinguishes **process simulation** (simulating the steps of use) from **outcome simulation** (simulating end-state benefits), and shows both the power and the boundary conditions of each.

---

## 3.1 Taylor, Pham, Rivkin & Armor (1998) — the process/outcome distinction

**Citation:** Taylor, S. E., Pham, L. B., Rivkin, I. D., & Armor, D. A. (1998). Harnessing the imagination: Mental simulation, self-regulation, and coping. *American Psychologist*, 53(4), 429–439. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Program of studies (incl. the well-known exam-studying experiments) comparing students who simulated the *process* of achieving a goal (studying) vs. the *outcome* (getting an A) vs. controls.

**Effect sizes:** Process simulation improved planning, reduced anxiety, and improved actual performance (higher exam scores vs. outcome simulation and control); outcome simulation alone produced motivation without means, sometimes worse than control. The asymmetry — process beats outcome for behavior regulation — is one of the most cited findings in the simulation literature.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The foundational warning: pure benefit-fantasy content ("imagine never forgetting anything") produces desire without action-path, which in a pre-order context translates into wishlist-and-leave behavior. Every outcome vignette on the page should carry an embedded process spine — the *how* of the moment ("you tap it once, say 'remember this,' and go back to your conversation"). For conversion specifically, the checkout flow itself benefits from process simulation: a three-step visual ("Reserve today → app access tonight → pendant in August") is a process simulation of *buying*, which reduces the planning-anxiety component of hesitation. Post-order, process simulation of day-one use primes successful onboarding and reduces returns (Section 10).

## 3.2 Escalas (2004) — imagining yourself using the product: narrative self-referencing

**Citation:** Escalas, J. E. (2004). Imagine yourself in the product: Mental simulation, narrative transportation, and persuasion. *Journal of Advertising*, 33(2), 37–48. (Access: abstract + secondary sources.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments with ads prompting self-focused mental simulation ("imagine yourself...") vs. control framings; measured transportation, ad/brand attitudes, intentions; examined argument-strength moderation.

**Effect sizes:** Self-referencing simulation instructions increased narrative transportation and improved attitudes/intentions; notably, transportation *reduced* analytical elaboration — weak arguments were penalized less under simulation (attitudes driven by the quality of the imagined experience rather than argument scrutiny).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Two levers. (1) The literal phrase works: copy that begins "Imagine your morning..." in second person measurably shifts processing into simulation mode. The pendant's hero section should be a 3-beat second-person narrative, not a feature list. (2) The elaboration-suppression finding is strategically double-edged: simulation-heavy pages soften spec-scrutiny (helpful when the category is new and specs are provisional pre-production), but Section 4's ELM evidence says durable, high-commitment support needs central-route substance too. Resolution: simulation first (hero), substance beneath (spec/credential blocks) — matching how scrollers actually allocate attention, and letting each processing style find its food. Never *replace* verifiable claims with vibes: pre-order buyers burned by vaporware are precisely the skeptical elaborators the narrative won't catch.

## 3.3 Zhao, Hoeffler & Dahl (2009) — visualization for really new products

**Citation:** Zhao, M., Hoeffler, S., & Dahl, D. W. (2009). The role of imagination-focused visualization on new product evaluation. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 46(1), 46–55. (Access: abstract + secondary sources.) See also Hoeffler, S. (2003). Measuring preferences for really new products. *JMR*, 40(4), 406–420.

**Sample/method:** Experiments on really-new-product (RNP) evaluation comparing imagination-focused visualization (imagining one's own usage situations) vs. memory-focused strategies, with instruction-based manipulations.

**Effect sizes:** For RNPs, guided imagination-focused visualization improved evaluations and preference stability vs. unguided or memory-anchored processing; for incrementally new products, memory-based anchoring sufficed (interaction effects). Hoeffler (2003): preference measurement for RNPs improves when consumers are helped to simulate usage (e.g., scenario provision), reducing preference uncertainty.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Do not ask cold visitors to imagine unaided — an AI pendant triggers no usable memory schema, and unguided imagination produces noise or sci-fi anxieties (surveillance tropes). Provide *scaffolded* visualization: pre-written, selectable usage scenarios ("In meetings," "With my kids," "Traveling," "Studying") that expand into concrete first-person vignettes; the visitor picks the ones that match their life, which simultaneously scaffolds imagination (this paper) and constitutes self-product elaboration (Kim & Hedgcock, 1.8). The scenario-picker's selections are also first-party data for segmenting the 12-month nurture stream by imagined use case.

## 3.4 Elder & Krishna (2012) — the visual depiction effect: orientation enables embodied simulation

**Citation:** Elder, R. S., & Krishna, A. (2012). The "visual depiction effect" in advertising: Facilitating embodied mental simulation through product orientation. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 38(6), 988–1003. (Access: abstract + secondary sources.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments varying product image orientation (e.g., mug handle facing the viewer's dominant hand vs. away), measuring purchase intentions; moderators: dominant hand, imagined hand occupancy.

**Effect sizes:** Orienting products toward the viewer's dominant hand increased purchase intentions via embodied mental simulation of grasping; the effect *reversed or vanished* when the dominant hand was imagined occupied — demonstrating the mechanism is motor simulation, with intention differences on the order of half a scale point in the reported studies.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Product photography is a simulation instrument with settable parameters. Render the pendant (a) at graspable scale and angle, clasp toward the viewer, (b) mid-action — being clipped on, thumb on its surface — because images of interaction states trigger motor simulation better than museum shots, and (c) right-hand-dominant by default (≈90% of viewers). Avoid hero images where hands are visibly *occupied with a phone* while the pendant is worn — per the occupied-hand moderator this actively suppresses simulated interaction with the pendant. For the app screenshots, show a thumb mid-tap rather than sterile screens.

## 3.5 Displaying the last stage before consumption (2024) — LSC imagery drives simulation (FULL-TEXT SOURCE, abstract-level detail)

**Citation:** (Authors per journal record) The influence of displaying the last stage before consumption on purchase intention: The mediating role of mental simulation. *Current Psychology* (2024), 10.1007/s12144-024-07067-1. (Access: abstract + article landing; Springer PDF fetch blocked.)

**Sample/method:** Five studies across product categories comparing ads displaying the *last stage before consumption* (LSC — e.g., food with a bite prepared, package opened) vs. complete/pristine product images.

**Effect sizes:** LSC display increased purchase intention, mediated by mental simulation of consuming/using the product; the effect held for hedonic products and not (or weaker) for purely functional ones.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The "last stage before consumption" for a pendant is the moment *just before first use*: box open, pendant lifted halfway out, chain uncoiled, phone showing the pairing screen at 90% — imagery of imminent use rather than completed styling. Since the effect concentrates in hedonic framing, pair LSC imagery with the pendant's hedonic register (delight, relief, connection) rather than productivity specs. A concrete asset list: (1) hero-adjacent photo of the opened founders' box; (2) a 6-second loop of the pendant being clasped; (3) app onboarding screen "Say hello to name-your-AI" — each an interrupted-action image that the viewer's simulation completes.

## 3.6 Yim, Kim & Lee (2021) — self-referencing image dynamics ease simulation

**Citation:** Yim, M. Y.-C., Kim, Y. K., & Lee, J. (2021). How to easily facilitate consumers' mental simulation through advertising: The effectiveness of self-referencing image dynamics on purchase intention. *International Journal of Advertising*, 40(5), 810–834. (Access: abstract + first-page excerpts.)

**Sample/method:** Multi-study experimental work manipulating self-referencing cues and image dynamics (implied motion) in ads; measured mental simulation and purchase intention; moderator: prior direct product experience.

**Effect sizes:** Self-referencing cues increased ease of mental simulation and (marginally to significantly) purchase intention; prior direct experience moderated the simulation link (experienced consumers simulate easily without cues), not the intention link.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The moderator is the actionable part: cues that scaffold simulation matter *most for the inexperienced* — which for an unreleased RNP is everyone. Use implied-motion assets (pendant mid-swing on its chain, conversation waveform animating) and self-referencing text overlays ("your 9am standup, remembered") rather than static catalog imagery. As real owners exist post-August 2026, the calculus flips — UGC of actual use will outperform constructed simulation cues; plan the asset roadmap accordingly (constructed simulation 2025–26, harvested reality after ship).

## 3.7 Mental simulation against fear of new technology (2021) — MDPI upcycled-food studies

**Citation:** (Authors per journal record) Using imagination to overcome fear: How mental simulation nudges consumers' purchase intentions for upcycled food. *Sustainability*, 13(3), 1130 (2021). (Access: abstract + partial full text.)

**Sample/method:** Three scenario-based online experiments using mental simulation to counter unfamiliarity/technology fear for upcycled food (an aversive-novelty category), measuring product evaluation and purchase intention.

**Effect sizes:** Mental simulation (esp. outcome-flavored, benefit-focused) significantly improved evaluations and purchase intentions relative to no-simulation controls, attenuating novelty-fear (exact stats at abstract level).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The pendant's analogue of "technology fear" is the always-listening privacy anxiety — the single most predictable objection for an AI wearable. This work suggests simulation can *metabolize* the fear rather than merely answering it with policy text: a guided simulation of the privacy experience itself ("You tap it off. The light dies. Nothing has left the device — replay what it heard, delete it, watch it forget") converts an abstract dread into a concrete, controllable interaction. Put a privacy *interaction demo* — not a privacy *policy link* — in the objection zone of the page.

## 3.8 Cross-reference: Castaño et al. (2008) — matching simulation type to temporal distance

Covered in full at **2.4**. The scheduling rule it contributes to this section: **outcome simulation while shipping is distant; process simulation as it nears.** Combined with Taylor et al. (3.1), the refined rule is: outcome simulation *with embedded process spine* early; explicit process simulation late; never outcome-only at any stage.

## 3.9 Section synthesis — the simulation toolkit ranked

1. **Scaffolded scenario-picker** (3.3 + 1.8): guided usage vignettes the visitor selects — simultaneously simulation aid, elaboration inducer, and segmentation data. Highest expected ROI; direct experimental analogues in two literatures.
2. **Second-person narrative hero with process spine** (3.1, 3.2): "Imagine..." + one-tap mechanics inside each vignette.
3. **Simulation-engineered visuals** (3.4, 3.5, 3.6): dominant-hand orientation, interrupted-action LSC frames, implied motion.

5. **Buying-process simulation** (3.1): reserve → app tonight → pendant August graphic at checkout.

# SECTION 4 — Elaboration Likelihood in Crowdfunding (Allison et al. and successors)

## 4.0 Framing: a pre-order page is a persuasion event with two doors

The Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) holds that persuasion travels through a **central route** (effortful scrutiny of issue-relevant arguments; attitudes formed this way are durable and behavior-predictive) or a **peripheral route** (low-effort reliance on cues — source attractiveness, consensus counts, tone; attitudes formed this way are weaker but cheaply obtained), with the route determined by the receiver's **motivation** and **ability** to elaborate. Crowdfunding pages — and by extension pre-order pages — are natural ELM laboratories: they carry both dense issue-relevant information and abundant cues, and their audiences vary enormously in expertise and stakes.

---

## 4.1 Petty & Cacioppo (1986) — the model itself

**Citation:** Petty, R. E., & Cacioppo, J. T. (1986). The elaboration likelihood model of persuasion. In *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* (Vol. 19, pp. 123–205). (Access: canonical theory; treated in depth in the full text of Allison et al. 2017.)

**Sample/method:** Theoretical integration over a decade of persuasion experiments (argument quality × involvement × cue manipulations).

**Effect sizes:** The signature crossover: under high involvement, argument quality dominates attitudes and cues fade; under low involvement, cues dominate and argument quality fades. Central-route attitudes persist longer, resist counter-persuasion, and predict behavior better.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** A $149.99, 12-month-wait purchase is *high-involvement for the buyer at the moment of decision* but most sessions begin at low involvement (ad click, 8-second scan). So the page must sequence routes: peripheral cues (design quality, backer count, press logos, founder face) win the first 10 seconds and *raise motivation to elaborate*; central content (how memory capture works, battery/specs, privacy architecture, founder track record) then builds the durable attitude that survives a 12-month wait full of second thoughts. The ELM's durability asymmetry is the key point for pre-orders specifically: **a conversion won on peripheral cues alone is a future cancellation** — the attitude decays before August 2026. Budget page depth accordingly: cue-rich surface, argument-rich depth, and lifecycle emails that keep feeding the central route during the wait.

---

## 4.2 Allison, Davis, Webb & Short (2017) — the ELM tested on 383 Kickstarter ventures + a conjoint experiment (FULL TEXT READ — centerpiece)

**Citation:** Allison, T. H., Davis, B. C., Webb, J. W., & Short, J. C. (2017). Persuasion in crowdfunding: An elaboration likelihood model of crowdfunding performance. *Journal of Business Venturing*, 32(6), 707–725. (Access: full text, author-hosted PDF, read in full.)

**Sample/method:** Study 1: content analysis + logistic regression on 383 Kickstarter ventures (random sample drawn Nov 2011; representativeness checked by t-tests against the platform population; industry/location dummies; robust SEs). Central-route (issue-relevant) variables: entrepreneur education (H1), functional experience (H2), ingredient branding (H3), product interconnections/complementarities (H4). Peripheral cues: personal-dream framing (H5), group identity language (H6), positive tone (H7). Moderators: funding commitment size (motivation, H8) and funder experience (ability, H9). Study 2: metric conjoint decision experiment, N = 150 participants × 16 profiles = 2,351 rated profiles.

**Effect sizes (Study 1, logistic regression on funded/not):**
- Education: **b = −0.75, p = .016** — *negative*; H1 rejected. Post-hoc: for very high funding commitments (rewards ≥ $100, the top ~1% of projects), the sign flips positive (b = 1.27, p = .029) — credentials only get processed when stakes are high.
- Functional experience: b = 0.62, p = .037 (supported).
- Ingredient branding (named component/partner brands): **b = 2.41, p < .001** — among the largest effects.
- Product interconnections (works with existing ecosystems): **b = 3.05, p < .001** — the largest effect in the model.
- Personal-dream framing: b = 1.00, p = .004. Group identity ("join us," "we"): b = 1.65, p = .008. Positive tone: ns (b = 0.07, p = .155).
- Moderation: low funding commitments strengthen peripheral-cue effects (e.g., personal dream × low commitment b = 0.96, p = .040; group identity similar); high commitments/experience strengthen issue-relevant information (partial support; several interactions p = .103–.175).
- Study 2 (conjoint): central-route information robust (education b = 0.20–0.30 with commitment/experience moderators, p ≤ .024; related experience × commitment b = 0.61 and × experience b = 0.71, both p < .001; ingredient branding × commitment b = 0.419, p < .001); peripheral cues *reversed* in the stripped-down lab context (personal dream b = −0.15, p = .004) — the authors attribute this to lost realism, a caution that cue effects are context-dependent.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The two monster coefficients are directly actionable. (1) **Ingredient branding (b = 2.41):** name the components — the titanium alloy grade and supplier story, the chip vendor, the mic array maker, the AI model partner ("powered by [model]"), assembly location. Every named, checkable ingredient is central-route ammunition that roughly doubled odds of funding in-context. (2) **Interconnections (b = 3.05):** show the pendant inside the buyer's existing ecosystem — iOS/Android apps, calendar, notes apps, WhatsApp export, smartwatch companion. An RNP that plugs into what people already use borrows feasibility and slashes hypothetical distance (Section 2). (3) **Group identity (b = 1.65) beats credentialism for the mass audience:** "Join 4,000 founding members" outperforms the founders' resumes for typical visitors — but the post-hoc education flip says the calculus reverses above ~$100 commitments, and anticipy.ai's $149.99 is *above* that threshold: unlike a $25 Kickstarter pledge, this audience is motivated to elaborate, so founder credentials, technical documentation, and named partners deserve prime placement, not footer burial. (4) **Personal-dream framing (b = 1.00) works on-platform but reversed in sterile contexts** — use founder-story framing in narrative environments (page story section, video, PR) and keep it out of transactional surfaces (checkout, cart emails) where it reads as unprofessional. (5) Positive tone was null — sunny adjectives are free but worthless; spend the words on ingredients and interconnects.

## 4.3 Allison, Davis, Short & Webb (2015) — intrinsic vs. extrinsic cue framing in prosocial lending (185,289 loans)

**Citation:** Allison, T. H., Davis, B. C., Short, J. C., & Webb, J. W. (2015). Crowdfunding in a prosocial microlending environment: Examining the role of intrinsic versus extrinsic cues. *Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice*, 39(1), 53–73. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries; the companion study frequently paired with 4.2.)

**Sample/method:** Computerized text analysis of the narratives of ~185k Kiva microloans, relating linguistic framing (opportunity/business language vs. human-interest/help language) to funding speed.

**Effect sizes:** Loans framed as opportunities to *help another person* funded significantly faster than loans framed as business opportunities (intrinsic cues positive, extrinsic/business cues negative for lender response); effects significant across the very large sample (exact coefficients not retrieved at abstract level).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Even in reward contexts, backers respond to being cast as *helpers in a story*, not investors in a transaction. anticipy.ai's pre-order narrative should include an honest help-frame: "Pre-orders fund our first production run — you're not just buying early, you're the reason a small team gets to build this without VC strings." This frame (a) recruits the prosocial motive documented across Section 6's motivation literature, (b) reframes the 12-month wait as participation rather than deprivation, and (c) inoculates against delay frustration — people forgive partners more readily than vendors (see Section 10). Boundary: the help-frame must coexist with competence signals; helping-framing without capability evidence reads as charity risk at $149.99.

## 4.4 Bi, Liu & Usman (2017) — ELM applied to on-page information and e-WOM in Chinese crowdfunding

**Citation:** Bi, S., Liu, Z., & Usman, K. (2017). The influence of online information on investing decisions of reward-based crowdfunding. *Journal of Business Research*, 71, 10–18. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries; 500+ citations.)

**Sample/method:** Data from Chinese reward-based crowdfunding projects; models quality of project information (word counts of introductions, video counts — central route) and e-WOM volume (numbers of "likes" and comments — peripheral route) against funding outcomes.

**Effect sizes:** Both central-route information quantity/quality and peripheral e-WOM volume significantly increased funding (positive, significant coefficients across models); e-WOM effects were substantial for reward-based projects, consistent with low-elaboration processing among small backers.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Comment/like counters are not vanity metrics — they are peripheral-route persuasion infrastructure. The pre-order page should carry living social-exhaust surfaces: a public FAQ/AMA thread with visible activity, review widgets for the companion app, X/Reddit embed of real discussion, running backer counter. Two design rules follow from the ELM mechanics: (a) surface *volume* cues prominently (counts, recency timestamps) because volume is what low-elaboration visitors read; (b) curate *content* quality of the top-visible comments because high-elaboration visitors read those as arguments. Cold-start problem before volume exists: substitute press logos, waitlist size ("12,400 on the waitlist"), and founder-answered questions.

## 4.5 Parhankangas & Renko (2017) — linguistic style: it's how you say it, for whom

**Citation:** Parhankangas, A., & Renko, M. (2017). Linguistic style and crowdfunding success among social and commercial entrepreneurs. *Journal of Business Venturing*, 32(2), 215–236. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Computer-aided text analysis (LIWC) of ~656 Kickstarter campaigns (social vs. commercial), relating linguistic style dimensions (concreteness, precision, interactivity, psychological distancing) to funding success.

**Effect sizes:** For *social* campaigns, linguistic styles that make pitches more understandable and relatable (concrete language, precision, interactive style, low psychological distancing) significantly boosted success; for *commercial* campaigns, linguistic style mattered much less — content/product substance dominated (significant style × campaign-type interactions).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** anticipy.ai is a commercial campaign, so per this evidence the *substance* (product, specs, credibility) will dominate style — do not expect copywriting finesse to rescue a weak evidence layer. But the pendant's narrative has a social-adjacent layer (small team, privacy mission, human-memory mission), and wherever that layer speaks (founder letter, mission section, updates), the social-campaign style rules apply: concrete words, numeric precision ("11 g," "4 mics," "August 2026," never "soon"), direct address ("you"), present tense. A practical hybrid: run the substance sections like a commercial spec sheet and the mission sections like a social campaign — and measure both with LIWC-style tooling (cross-reference 2.6).

## 4.6 Section synthesis — an ELM blueprint for the anticipy.ai page

**Route-mapping the audience.** At $149.99 (above Allison's ~$100 elaboration threshold), the *decision* will be centrally processed by most buyers — but *attention* is still won peripherally. Design as a funnel through the ELM: cues earn seconds, arguments earn dollars.

**Peripheral layer (first viewport, ambient):** backer/waitlist counter; press and award logos; founder faces; polished motion design (design quality is itself a peripheral competence cue); group-identity CTA ("Join the founding 5,000"); visible community activity (4.4).

**Central layer (scroll depth, expandable):** ingredient-brand block naming titanium grade, chip, mics, AI partner (b = 2.41 analogue); ecosystem/interconnect diagram (b = 3.05 analogue); founder experience dossier (functional experience b = 0.62; education signals now valuable at this price point per the post-hoc flip); technical white paper and privacy architecture doc as downloadable PDFs (the existence of deep documents is itself persuasive; their content serves true elaborators).

**Route-durability plan for the 12-month wait:** schedule central-route feed (engineering updates, third-party validations, teardown videos) at least monthly, because peripherally-persuaded buyers churn when cues fade and no argument-based attitude took root. Track leading indicator: update-email click-through on technical content as a proxy for centrally-anchored commitment.

# SECTION 5 — Reward-Tier Design

## 5.0 Framing: the tier menu is simultaneously price discrimination, signaling, and community architecture

Crowdfunding campaigns rarely sell one thing at one price; they sell a *menu* of reward tiers. The literature approaches tier design from three angles: analytical (optimal menu/pricing under all-or-nothing mechanics), empirical (which reward structures correlate with success), and behavioral (what non-material rewards do to backers). A pre-order page is a degenerate campaign — often one SKU — but every insight below transfers to the design of its offer stack (variants, bundles, founder editions, add-ons).

---

## 5.1 Hu, Li & Shi (2015) — the theory of optimal crowdfunding menus (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Hu, M., Li, X., & Shi, M. (2015). Product and pricing decisions in crowdfunding. *Marketing Science*, 34(3), 331–345. (Access: full text, University of Toronto repository PDF.)

**Sample/method:** Game-theoretic model of a creator selling to sequentially arriving buyers under an all-or-nothing funding goal; buyers' valuations heterogeneous; extensions cover altruistic buyers, no-shows, strategic waiting, and multiple projects (separate vs. bundle funding).

**Effect sizes/key results (analytical):** (1) Because early pledgers' commitments raise the probability the project succeeds, buyers exert *pledge externalities* on each other; the creator optimally offers a **menu of at least two quality/price levels** more often than a classic monopolist would — menus are "win-win," raising both expected profit and success probability. (2) The optimal menu features **larger quality spread and compressed price spread** relative to standard second-degree price discrimination: the high tier is relatively underpriced to recruit the pivotal enthusiasts whose pledges de-risk the campaign for everyone else. (3) Menu pricing with separate funding of components can beat bundling by spreading risk. (4) Results survive altruism and strategic waiting.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Even as a self-hosted pre-order (no explicit funding goal), the pledge-externality logic holds psychologically: visible early commitment makes later buyers more confident (cf. 4.4, 6.x social proof). Design implications: (1) offer at least two tiers — e.g., Standard ($149.99) and a Founders' Edition (numbered engraving, extra chain, lifetime Memory Vault, priority shipping batch) — because a menu recruits the enthusiasts whose visible purchases carry the campaign; (2) per the compressed-price-spread result, price the Founders' Edition *less* above cost than intuition suggests (e.g., $189, not $249): its job is not margin but recruiting conspicuous, high-commitment early adopters whose existence reassures the marginal $149.99 buyer; (3) if accessories exist (extra chains, charging dock), sell them as separate add-ons rather than forcing bundles — separate funding spreads perceived risk and lowers the entry commitment.

---

## 5.2 Thürridl & Kamleitner (2016) — the taxonomy of crowdfunding rewards

**Citation:** Thürridl, C., & Kamleitner, B. (2016). What goes around comes around? Rewards as strategic assets in crowdfunding. *California Management Review*, 58(2), 88–110. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Qualitative/typological analysis of reward structures across large samples of campaigns, developing a taxonomy: product-based rewards (the item itself, early/exclusive versions), experience-based rewards (visits, participation, co-creation), recognition-based rewards (names in credits, community status), and their strategic functions (financing, marketing, relationship-building).

**Effect sizes:** Typological work; no pooled effect sizes. Key qualitative finding: non-product rewards (experience, recognition) are cheap to provide, hard to price-compare, and build relational assets — but only product rewards drive volume.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The tier stack should be built as *product core + experience/recognition shell*. The $149.99 tier carries the product; the shell adds near-zero-marginal-cost items with high perceived and relational value: name in the "Founding 5,000" credits page (recognition), invitation to monthly build-update calls with the founders (experience), vote rights on roadmap features (co-creation, cf. 1.14), early beta access to the app (experience + utility). Because these are non-comparable, they blunt price comparison with future retail and with competitor gadgets — nobody can price-match "your name etched in the launch archive." The taxonomy also warns against reward bloat: each added physical reward (t-shirts, stickers) adds fulfillment risk and delay (Mollick, Section 6.4/10), while each added experiential/recognition reward adds almost none.

---

## 5.3 Kuppuswamy & Bayus — backer dynamics: the bathtub, the deadline effect, and goal proximity (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Kuppuswamy, V., & Bayus, B. L. (2018). Crowdfunding creative ideas: The dynamics of project backers. In *The Economics of Crowdfunding* (pp. 151–182). Palgrave. Working-paper version: "Crowdfunding creative ideas: The dynamics of project backers in Kickstarter" (UNC, read in full). Companion: Kuppuswamy, V., & Bayus, B. L. (2017). Does my contribution to your crowdfunding project matter? *Journal of Business Venturing*, 32(1), 72–89. (Access: full text of working paper; JBV companion at abstract level.)

**Sample/method:** Two years of panel data on Kickstarter projects (successfully and unsuccessfully funded; representative project has ~$12.7k funding), modeling weekly backer support over the funding cycle.

**Effect sizes:** (1) Backer support is **"bathtub"-shaped** — high in the first and last weeks, low in the middle — unlike the monotone herding patterns in equity/lending crowdfunding. (2) Mid-campaign slump is driven by **diffusion of responsibility**: additional support *decreases* when a project has already received substantial backing, unless the goal is nearly reached (bystander effect among potential backers); support re-accelerates near the deadline (deadline effect) and near the goal (impact perception — "my contribution matters"). (3) Project updates spike in the final week and are associated with successful closes.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** An open-ended pre-order page has no deadline and no goal — which per this evidence forfeits the two strongest natural accelerants of commitment and invites indefinite deferral ("I can order any time" = the mid-campaign slump, permanently). Reintroduce both, honestly: (1) **batch deadlines** — "Founding batch (5,000 units, August 2026 delivery) closes March 31 or when allocated; batch 2 delivers Q4 2026" — creating recurring deadline effects with real fulfillment logic behind them; (2) **goal-proximity displays** — "4,312 / 5,000 founding units claimed" — which flips social proof from a bystander cue ("plenty of others are handling this") into an impact/urgency cue ("nearly gone, my order completes the batch"). The bystander finding is subtle and crucial: raw popularity counters can *suppress* action when they imply the project is safely successful without you; always pair the count with a capacity limit so the visitor's own order remains pivotal.

---

## 5.4 Pinkow (2023) — inside an overfunded campaign: what supporters say moved them (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Pinkow, F. (2023). Determinants of overfunding in reward-based crowdfunding. *Electronic Commerce Research*, 25, 155–192. (Access: full text, Springer open access.)

**Sample/method:** Qualitative critical-case study of a massively overfunded project (with reference to cases like Star Citizen), expert interviews with supporters and team members, three-phase process model (crowd, project, communication characteristics).

**Effect sizes:** Qualitative; no effect sizes. Converging themes: the **central role of the founder** (visibility, credibility, responsiveness); communication cadence and transparency as the engine of continued/increased pledges after the goal is met; community identity ("being part of it") sustaining pledges long past rational need; word-of-mouth from engaged supporters as the main acquisition channel in later phases.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Overfunding dynamics are the model for what a *rolling* pre-order campaign should aspire to after initial traction: the founder as recurring on-camera presence (build-log videos, AMA replies under their own name), a cadence contract ("progress update on the first Monday of every month — here's the archive"), and community surfaces where being early is an identity ("Founding member #212" badges in the app/forum). Pinkow's supporters explicitly kept spending *because* communication made progress feel shared; for anticipy.ai the analogues are add-on purchases (second chain, gift units) and referrals during the wait — design the update emails with a soft commerce/referral module so sustained engagement has a transactional outlet.

## 5.5 Crosetto & Regner (2018) — self-funding, early pledges, and the pre-order character of reward crowdfunding

**Citation:** Crosetto, P., & Regner, T. (2018). It's never too late: Funding dynamics and self-pledges in reward-based crowdfunding. *Research Policy*, 47(8), 1463–1477. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Analysis of ~2,000+ campaigns on Startnext (Germany's largest reward platform) with unusually granular pledge-level data, including self-pledges by founders and pledge timing; examines what share of pledges are effectively pre-orders.

**Effect sizes:** A large majority of reward-crowdfunding pledges are *de facto pre-orders* (backers selecting product-delivering tiers rather than donation tiers); early momentum strongly predicts success, but — the paper's signature finding — late surges can rescue initially slow campaigns (unlike platforms where early failure is terminal); self-pledges/insider pledges function as seeding.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Confirmation that the crowdfunding literature *is* the pre-order literature: most backers are buying products, so everything in Sections 4–6 transfers. Tactically: (1) seeding is legitimate mechanics, not just vanity — arrange for the first visible wave (team, friends-and-family, waitlist VIPs given 24-hour early access) so the public counter never reads embarrassingly low (cf. bystander/social-proof dynamics in 5.3); (2) "never too late": maintain conversion infrastructure for late-cycle surges (press moments, review embargo drops near ship date) rather than assuming the launch-week cohort is the whole market — the August 2026 shipping announcement itself is a plannable late surge.

# SECTION 6 — Backer Motivation: Systematic Reviews and Foundational Studies

## 6.0 Framing: why people give money to unfinished things

If Section 1 explains the *pull* of pre-orders (claiming ownership), this section maps the whole motivational field — including motives a plain e-commerce mindset misses (helping, belonging, participating) and the deterrents (distrust, fear of non-delivery) that a 12-month wait amplifies.

---

## 6.1 Gerber & Hui (2013) — the foundational motivations/deterrents taxonomy

**Citation:** Gerber, E. M., & Hui, J. (2013). Crowdfunding: Motivations and deterrents for participation. *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*, 20(6), Article 34. (Access: abstract + extensive secondary coverage; 384+ citations; companion CSCW/CHI papers.)

**Sample/method:** Qualitative interview study (semi-structured interviews with creators and supporters across major platforms; grounded-theory analysis).

**Effect sizes:** Qualitative taxonomy (no effect sizes). Supporter motivations: (1) **collect rewards** (the product — described by many as "shopping for the future"); (2) **help others** / support creators; (3) **be part of a community** of like-minded people; (4) **support a cause/values**. Supporter deterrent, singular and dominant: **distrust of creators' use of funds and ability to deliver**. Creator motivations include raising funds while *expanding awareness, forming connections, gaining approval, and maintaining control*.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The taxonomy is a completeness checklist for the page. Motivation (1) is the buy box; (2) requires an honest help-frame ("pre-orders fund the first production run of an independent team" — cf. 4.3); (3) requires visible community (founding-member badge, forum/Discord, update comment threads); (4) requires an articulated cause (privacy-respecting AI, screen-free presence, human memory). The deterrent side is even more actionable: since *distrust of delivery* is the No. 1 reason people who want the product don't back, the page needs a dedicated "Will this actually ship?" block — production partner named, tooling photos, team hardware track record, refund-until-ship guarantee (Section 9), and the August 2026 date stated with unusual specificity. Treat that block as seriously as the hero.

## 6.2 Steigenberger (2017) — three motive classes for reward backers

**Citation:** Steigenberger, N. (2017). Why supporters contribute to reward-based crowdfunding. *International Journal of Entrepreneurial Behavior & Research*, 23(2), 336–353. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Survey-based study of reward-crowdfunding supporters, testing the relative weight of purchasing, altruism, and involvement/gaming motives.

**Effect sizes:** All three motive classes significant: **purchase motivation dominates** (backers are primarily early customers), but **altruistic** and **involvement** ("being part of the journey," entertainment value of following the project) motives contribute *incrementally and independently* to pledge behavior; involvement motives are especially characteristic of repeat backers.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Quantitative confirmation that the pre-order should be *sold as a purchase but wrapped as a journey*. The purchase layer must stand on its own ($149.99, specs, date, refund). The wrapper — build-log content, live milestones, member votes — is not decoration; it independently adds pledge propensity, and it is what repeat early-adopters (the highest-LTV, highest-WOM segment) come for. Practical test: every update email should answer both "what did you build?" (involvement) and "what does this mean for my unit?" (purchase) in its first two lines.

## 6.3 Popescul, Radu, Păvăloaia & Georgescu (2020) — systematic review of psychological determinants (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Popescul, D., Radu, L.-D., Păvăloaia, V.-D., & Georgescu, M. R. (2020). Psychological determinants of investor motivation in social media-based crowdfunding projects: A systematic review. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 11, 588121. (Access: full text, Frontiers open access.)

**Sample/method:** PRISMA-style systematic review: 1,216 publications identified across IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Scopus → 515 screened → **78 articles** read in depth; findings clustered into general studies, determinants of investor behavior, and campaign success factors.

**Effect sizes:** Review-level synthesis (no pooled quantitative effects). Determinant clusters: intrinsic motives (altruism, belonging, identification with the project/creator, enjoyment), extrinsic motives (rewards, deals/early access), trust (in creator, platform, and technology) as the pivotal enabling condition, plus social-media affordances (interactivity, social proof, direct creator contact) that potentiate the psychological determinants.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Two structural lessons. (1) Trust is positioned as the *gate*, not one factor among many: motivational copy is wasted on visitors who haven't cleared the credibility threshold — sequence the page so baseline trust artifacts (team, partners, press, guarantee) appear before the deep pitch, and instrument trust separately (e.g., exit surveys asking "what would you need to see to order?"). (2) The affordance findings argue for making the pre-order page *social-media-shaped*: creator replies in public, embedded real posts, reactive updates — the psychological determinants fire hardest when the medium enables perceived direct access to the makers. A sterile Shopify template discards precisely the affordances this review credits with potentiating backer psychology.

## 6.4 Mollick (2014) — the empirical baseline: quality signals, networks, and endemic delays (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Mollick, E. (2014). The dynamics of crowdfunding: An exploratory study. *Journal of Business Venturing*, 29(1), 1–16. (Access: full text PDF mirror, read in full.)

**Sample/method:** 48,500+ Kickstarter projects (~$237M); logistic regressions of success determinants; hand-collected delivery-tracking subsample (247 delivered projects) with Cox proportional-hazard modeling of delays.

**Effect sizes (from the full text):** (1) Success is bimodal: failures fail big (mean failed project raises 10.3% of goal; only 3% of failures reach 50%), successes succeed small (25% of funded projects at ≤3% over goal; 50% within ~10%). (2) Quality signals move odds massively: for an average film project, having a video raises modeled success chance from 15% to 37%; spelling errors, missing videos, and no updates all significantly predict failure. (3) Founder network size (Facebook friends) strongly predicts success. (4) Delivery: **over 75% of product-delivering projects shipped late**; among the 247 delivered, mean delay 1.28 months (SD 1.56), and delay severity *increases with funding level* — overfunded projects are the most delayed. (5) Geography matters (projects cluster with local cultural products).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Three transfers. (1) *Execution quality is read as delivery probability:* typos, stock renders, or a missing product video are processed by visitors as non-delivery risk, not aesthetic lapses — invest in a real filmed product video (the single strongest signal in the data) and copy-edit ruthlessly. (2) *The delay base rate is public knowledge:* experienced backers assume hardware ships late because it usually does; anticipy.ai should out-signal the base rate rather than ignore it — publish the production timeline with buffer already included ("we set August 2026 with 3 months of buffer built in; here's the Gantt"), because a date that survives is worth more than an optimistic date that slips (Section 10 covers the dissonance cost of slippage). (3) *Network effects:* the founder's own network seeds the first wave (cf. 5.5) — plan the launch around the team's aggregate reach, waitlist, and any communities where the founders are already credible.

## 6.5 Strohmaier, Zeng & Hafeez (2019) — distrust is not the absence of trust

**Citation:** Strohmaier, D., Zeng, J., & Hafeez, M. (2019). Trust, distrust, and crowdfunding: A study on perceptions of institutional mechanisms. *Telematics and Informatics*, 43, 101252. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Survey study modeling trust and distrust (as separate constructs) toward founders-as-a-group and toward the platform, plus institutional protective mechanisms (platform rules, monitoring, pledging security), against individuals' funding behavior.

**Effect sizes:** Trust and distrust operate as **distinct constructs with independent effects** (not two ends of one scale); distrust in founders is a significant suppressor of funding behavior even controlling for trust; institutional mechanisms (rules, monitoring, security of pledges) mitigate distrust. (Exact coefficients at abstract level.)

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Building trust and dismantling distrust are *two different workstreams*. Trust-building: credentials, partners, press, polish (Sections 4, 6.4). Distrust-dismantling requires *institutional* answers the buyer doesn't have to take your word for: payment held via a recognized processor with buyer protection; an explicit contractual refund right until shipment (not a promise — a term of sale); third-party escrow or at least a named fulfillment partner; visible company registration and address; independent coverage. On-page, separate the two: a "Why trust us" block (positive signals) and a "What if it goes wrong" block (protective mechanisms). Omitting the second because it "raises negative thoughts" is exactly backwards per this evidence — unaddressed distrust suppresses funding independently of how much positive trust you pile up.

## 6.6 Zhao & Ryu (2020) and Shneor, Zhao & Flåten (eds.) — the reward-crowdfunding research synthesis (FULL TEXT READ, chapter)

**Citation:** Zhao, L., & Ryu, S. (2020). Reward-based crowdfunding research and practice. In Shneor, R., Zhao, L., & Flåten, B.-T. (Eds.), *Advances in Crowdfunding: Research and Practice* (pp. 119–143). Palgrave Macmillan (open access; chapter read in full). See also Shneor, R., & Munim, Z. H. (2019). Reward crowdfunding contribution as planned behaviour. *Journal of Business Research*, 103, 56–70.

**Sample/method:** Narrative synthesis of the reward-crowdfunding literature across disciplines (management, IS, marketing, entrepreneurship), organizing evidence on backers, creators, platforms, and campaigns; incorporates Shneor & Munim's TPB-based survey modeling (intention → behavior paths).

**Effect sizes:** Synthesis chapter. Notable relayed findings: information-sharing intention and financial-contribution intention *both* lead to actual contribution behavior (Shneor & Munim 2019); geographical/cultural distance and non-delivery risk are the persistent frictions; repeat-backer communities form around platforms and categories.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The sharing/contribution dual-path finding upgrades the referral program from marketing add-on to conversion mechanism: visitors not ready to buy should be offered a *sharing action* (waitlist + share for early-access position) because sharing intention is itself a pathway into eventual contribution — the person who shares anticipy.ai has begun backing it. Design the funnel with two exits from every page state: order (contribution path) or join-and-share (information path), and nurture the second toward the first with the involvement content of 6.2.

# SECTION 7 — Advance Selling Economics; Deposit vs. Full-Payment Pre-Orders

## 7.0 Framing: when should a seller sell early, and how should the buyer pay?

Two literatures meet here. The *advance selling* literature (marketing science / operations) explains when selling before the consumption date raises profit and how to price it. The *mental accounting* literature (behavioral economics) explains how the **timing and structure of payment** — full prepayment, deposit + balance, installments — changes the psychology of the purchase and of the eventual consumption. Together they answer anticipy.ai's most concrete structural question: should the pre-order be $149.99 now, a deposit now + balance at ship, or something else?

---

## 7.1 Xie & Shugan (2001) — the theory of advance selling (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Xie, J., & Shugan, S. M. (2001). Electronic tickets, smart cards, and online prepayments: When and how to advance sell. *Marketing Science*, 20(3), 219–243. (Access: full text via Internet Archive Scholar / Wayback copy of the author-hosted PDF.)

**Sample/method:** Analytical model. Buyers face uncertainty about their *own future valuation* (state of the world at consumption time); sellers choose advance vs. spot prices; extensions cover limited capacity, second-period arrivals, refunds, buyer risk aversion, exogenous credit costs, and premium advance pricing.

**Effect sizes/key results:** (1) Advance selling requires only **buyer uncertainty about future valuations** — not industry-specific features; at the advance moment, buyers are homogeneous "expected-valuation" purchasers, letting the seller extract surplus akin to first-degree price discrimination. (2) Advance selling can **almost double profits** relative to spot-only selling even with no capacity constraints, because profits come from *more buyers being able to purchase* (not from exploiting buyer surplus) — it can raise social welfare. (3) Key limiting factors: **seller credibility** (buyers must believe the spot price won't be lower, and that delivery will occur) and **marginal costs** (high marginal costs can make advance discounts unprofitable). (4) Advance prices are usually discounts but can optimally be **premiums** when capacity is large-but-limited and marginal costs modest (buyers pay to guarantee availability). (5) Refund availability changes optimal advance pricing (refundable advance sales approximate options; see Section 9).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** This model dignifies the pre-order as strategy, not stopgap, and yields specific structure. (1) The pre-order price *should* differ from planned retail, and the direction depends on the scarcity story: with a capped founding batch (limited capacity, credible), theory permits even a *premium* — in practice, the standard play is a modest discount ($149.99 pre-order vs. $199 retail) which compensates delay-degraded WTP (1.11) while extracting the expected-valuation purchase before buyers learn their true August-2026 valuation. (2) *Price-path credibility is load-bearing:* an explicit commitment — "retail will be $199; pre-order pricing will never be undercut" — is required for the advance sale to clear, because a rational visitor who suspects future discounts waits (this is the credibility condition, and it should be stated in writing on the page). (3) The "buy now because your future self might want it" logic implies target messaging at *anticipated states* ("by next summer you'll wish you'd started capturing this year") — legitimate because the model's engine is precisely valuation uncertainty resolving over time.

---

## 7.2 Chu & Zhang (2011) — pre-orders with endogenous information: how much should you reveal before release?

**Citation:** Chu, L. Y., & Zhang, H. (2011). Optimal preorder strategy with endogenous information control. *Management Science*, 57(6), 1055–1077. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Analytical model where the seller controls both pre-order pricing and how much product information (valuation-resolving signal) to release before launch; buyers decide to pre-order under uncertainty or wait.

**Effect sizes/key results:** The seller's optimal information policy is *not* full disclosure: revealing less pre-launch keeps buyers at homogeneous expected valuations (favoring advance extraction), while revealing more lets high-valuation buyers self-identify but invites low-valuation buyers to exit; optimal strategy couples information control with a price path (frequently: limited pre-launch information + attractive pre-order price + fuller revelation at launch).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** A principled answer to "how much do we show before ship?" — the model says strategic *sequencing* of revelation is legitimate: sell the pre-order on the stable, high-confidence value core (memory capture, titanium hardware, privacy architecture) while holding fine-grained comparables (exhaustive spec sheets, benchmark comparisons vs. competitors) for nearer launch, when they'd otherwise give fence-sitters reasons to defer. Caution against overreach: Section 6's distrust findings and Mollick's quality-signal evidence mean *credibility-relevant* information (team, production, refund) must never be part of the withheld set — withhold only valuation-refining detail, never delivery-risk-relevant detail. Practically: a public spec page marked "final specs confirmed at production, [these 8 core specs] guaranteed minimums" both controls information and converts the residual uncertainty into a floor-guarantee.

---

## 7.3 Prelec & Loewenstein (1998) — the double-entry mental accounting of prepayment

**Citation:** Prelec, D., & Loewenstein, G. (1998). The red and the black: Mental accounting of savings and debt. *Marketing Science*, 17(1), 4–28. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries; heavily reproduced.)

**Sample/method:** Theory ("double-entry" mental accounting: consumption is enjoyed net of the *pain of paying*; payments hurt net of the thought of consumption) plus survey studies (e.g., preferences for prepaying vacations vs. paying after; framing of installment plans).

**Effect sizes:** Signature survey result: a strong majority prefer to **prepay a vacation** (so consumption arrives "as if free") while preferring to be **paid after** doing work; "prospective accounting": prepayment decouples — by consumption time the payment is psychologically amortized, making consumption feel costless; debt-financed consumption is dampened by attention to future payments.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The strongest behavioral argument for **full payment at pre-order** rather than deposit + balance: a customer who pays $149.99 in September 2025 receives, in August 2026, a pendant that feels *free* — the payment pain is a year amortized, and the unboxing is pure gain (this also boosts initial product satisfaction and review sentiment, Section 10). Conversely, a $29 deposit with $120.99 charged at ship inverts the psychology: the big payment lands *at the moment of maximum scrutiny*, re-coupling pain to the product's first impression and creating a natural cancellation checkpoint. If a lower entry price is needed for conversion, prefer **installments that complete well before shipping** (e.g., 3 × $50, done by month 3) over ship-time balances — same affordability, prepaid psychology preserved.

---

## 7.4 Gourville & Soman (1998; 2002) — payment depreciation and transaction decoupling

**Citation:** Gourville, J. T., & Soman, D. (1998). Payment depreciation: The behavioral effects of temporally separating payments from consumption. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 25(2), 160–174; and Soman, D., & Gourville, J. T. (2001). Transaction decoupling: How price bundling affects the decision to consume. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 38(1), 30–44. (Access: abstracts + canonical summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Field + lab studies: health-club attendance by payment schedule (annual vs. quarterly vs. monthly billing); theater/ski-pass usage by bundling; scenario experiments on sunk-cost pressure over time since payment.

**Effect sizes:** Health-club members' attendance spikes in the month of payment and **decays steadily until the next payment** (payment depreciation — the sunk payment loses motivational force over time); bundled/decoupled payments (multi-performance passes) produce lower per-event usage pressure than itemized payments. Gym usage patterns show payment-month attendance far exceeding later months (roughly halving over the cycle in the reported data).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Payment depreciation is *good news* for prepaid pre-orders on the dissonance front: by August 2026 the $149.99 is psychologically sunk-and-forgotten, so the delivered pendant is evaluated on its merits with house money rather than against a fresh $149.99 benchmark — one more reason to prefer prepayment over ship-time balances (7.3). The flip side: depreciation also erodes the *commitment pressure* to engage ("I paid for this, I should use it"), which for anticipy.ai means the app-onboarding energy is highest in the weeks right after purchase — front-load the setup asks (voice, memory profile, personalization; cf. 1.13) into the first 30 days post-order rather than saving them for pre-ship, when the payment's motivational force has depreciated away.

---

## 7.5 Nan, et al. (2023) — mental accounting by time dimension in advance selling (supply-chain modeling)

**Citation:** (Authors per journal record) Supply chain optimization considering consumers' mental accounting by time dimension in advance selling. *Journal of University of Science and Technology of China* (2023). (Access: partial full text / landing-page abstract.)

**Sample/method:** Analytical supply-chain model embedding time-dimension mental accounting (consumers book the prepayment and delayed consumption in temporally separated accounts) into advance-selling price/inventory optimization.

**Effect sizes/key results:** Incorporating mental accounting changes optimal advance-selling depth and timing versus classical models: because consumers discount prepayment pain against future consumption, the effective advance-price elasticity differs from spot elasticity, shifting optimal advance discounts (model-dependent magnitudes).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** A supporting brick: even operations models now price in the psychology of 7.3–7.4 — the advance buyer's price sensitivity is *not* the spot buyer's. Practically, this argues for testing pre-order price points independently of planned retail elasticity assumptions (the $149.99 pre-order buyer is buying "future consumption with amortized pain," a different demand curve than the $199 day-one retail buyer), and for treating the pre-order discount depth as an empirical A/B question rather than a fixed percentage convention.

---

## 7.6 Kim & Hedgcock supplementary — deposit framing did not move pre-order intent

**Citation:** Kim & Hedgcock (Section 1.8), Supplementary Study 1. (Access: full text.)

**Sample/method:** Experimental comparison including a deposit-style partial-payment pre-order framing vs. standard framing on pre-order intention.

**Effect sizes:** No significant effect of the deposit framing on pre-order intent — consistent with the paper's thesis that pre-ordering is driven by ownership-claiming rather than financing convenience.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Converging with 7.3–7.4 from the demand side: lowering the upfront ask via deposits does not reliably buy intent, because the binding constraint is psychological (claiming, trust, distance), not liquidity, for most of this price band. At $149.99 — an affordable-indulgence price point — the deposit's conversion upside is speculative while its downsides (ship-time payment pain, cancellation checkpoint, added billing complexity and failed-card churn) are documented. Full payment should be the default; if payment flexibility is offered at all, bury it as a secondary option (e.g., Shop Pay installments) rather than architecting the offer around it.

---

## 7.7 Section synthesis — the payment architecture decision

**Recommendation: single full payment of $149.99 at pre-order**, with optional third-party installments completing before ship, and a written price-path commitment ("retail $199; early price never undercut"). Rationale stack:

1. Advance extraction works when buyers purchase at expected valuation before uncertainty resolves (7.1) — requires the credible price path.
2. Prepayment decouples pain from consumption; the August 2026 unboxing arrives psychologically free (7.3), improving first impressions and reviews.
3. Payment depreciation neutralizes the sunk $149.99 by delivery (7.4) — but front-load engagement asks into the post-purchase month before motivation depreciates.
4. Deposits don't raise intent (7.6) and concentrate payment pain at the pre-ship checkpoint, manufacturing a cancellation moment (7.3).
5. Balance-at-ship schemes also create operational churn (expired cards, failed charges) at exactly the wrong time.
6. The refund right (Section 9) supplies the risk relief that deposits pretend to offer — refundability, not payment splitting, is the correct answer to "what if I change my mind?"

# SECTION 8 — Delivery-Date Framing, Waiting, and the Utility of Anticipation

## 8.0 Framing: the wait is a design surface

Between checkout and August 2026 lies up to a year of pure psychology. Three literatures govern it: (1) *temporal framing* — the same objective interval feels longer or shorter depending on how it is described; (2) *intertemporal choice* — how delay discounts value, and how framing modulates the discount; (3) *anticipation* — the hedonic value (positive or negative) of waiting itself. Together they determine how anticipy.ai should write, frame, and program the delivery date.

---

## 8.1 Sokolova et al. (2023) — the days-of-the-week effect in temporal judgments

**Citation:** Sokolova, T., et al. (2023). Days-of-the-week effect in temporal judgments. *Journal of Consumer Research* (author list per journal record). (Access: abstract + search-result summaries; full text not retrieved — evidence graded accordingly.)

**Sample/method:** Eight preregistered studies, total **N = 4,758**, examining how consumers judge durations of intervals described with dates vs. days of the week (e.g., delivery "by Friday" vs. "by June 9"), including consequences for delivery-option choices and willingness to pay for expedited shipping.

**Effect sizes:** Objectively identical intervals were judged reliably shorter/longer depending on framing (day-of-week framings within the week horizon compress perceived duration; calendar-date framings behave differently across week boundaries); framing shifted downstream choices (e.g., expedited-shipping uptake) significantly across the preregistered set. (Exact per-study statistics not available at abstract level.)

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Micro-framing of time is measurably choice-relevant even at the scale of days — and by extension the macro-framing of a 12-month wait deserves deliberate engineering. Concrete rules derived jointly with 8.2–8.3: state the delivery as a **calendar event** ("Ships August 2026") rather than a duration ("ships in 11 months"); when the ship window is near, switch to day-of-week framing ("arriving by Friday, August 14") to compress the final perceived wait; in checkout and confirmation surfaces, *never* auto-render countdowns in day units ("342 days") — large day-counts are the psychologically longest possible framing of the same fact.

---

## 8.2 LeBoeuf (2006) — discounting depends on whether you say dates or delays

**Citation:** LeBoeuf, R. A. (2006). Discount rates for time versus dates: The sensitivity of discounting to time-interval description. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(1), 59–72. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments eliciting intertemporal preferences with intervals described either as extents ("in 8 months") or as dates ("on March 1"); both directions (delaying receipts, expediting payments).

**Effect sizes:** Discount rates were **systematically higher when intervals were described as extents than as dates** — the same future reward looked less valuable when its distance was framed as a duration; date framing produced more patient, less steeply discounted valuations (significant framing main effects across studies).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Direct instruction for the buy box: "Ships August 2026" (date) preserves more of the pendant's present value in the visitor's mind than any duration equivalent, because duration framing activates steeper discounting of the delayed good. Extend the rule across the funnel: ads ("August 2026" not "next year"), FAQ ("your unit ships in August 2026," never "about 12 months after ordering"), and post-purchase updates (milestones dated on the calendar: "Production starts March 2026", "Units leave the factory July 2026"). The one legitimate use of duration framing is *retrospective*: "you've been a founding member for 6 months" (1.7's ownership clock) — durations lengthen perceived *elapsed* investment just as they lengthen perceived *prospective* waits.

---

## 8.3 Read, Frederick, Orsel & Rahman (2005) — "four score and seven years from now": the date/delay effect quantified

**Citation:** Read, D., Frederick, S., Orsel, B., & Rahman, J. (2005). Four score and seven years from now: The date/delay effect in temporal discounting. *Management Science*, 51(9), 1326–1335. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Intertemporal choice experiments; matched elicitation of discounting over intervals framed as delays vs. as calendar dates; tests of hyperbolic vs. subadditive discounting under each framing.

**Effect sizes:** Discount rates elicited under **delay framing were roughly double** those under date framing in the reported comparisons; date framing also flattened the characteristic hyperbolic pattern (discounting became closer to constant-rate), i.e., framing changed not just the level but the *shape* of time preference.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Independent replication (different lab, different method) of 8.2 — the date-vs-delay rule is one of the most secure prescriptions in this report. The "shape" finding adds a subtlety: under delay framing, discounting is hyperbolically front-loaded, so the *first months* of a stated wait do disproportionate damage ("11 months" feels catastrophically far because the near term is steeply discounted). Date framing linearizes this. Practical corollary: when the team must communicate a schedule slip (Section 10), express it as a revised date ("now shipping October 2026"), never as an added duration ("delayed by 2 months") — the same bad news is discounted more gently in date form, and this is also more honest-feeling (a date is a commitment; a delta is an admission).

---

## 8.4 Loewenstein (1987) — anticipation as consumption: the delayed kiss

**Citation:** Loewenstein, G. (1987). Anticipation and the valuation of delayed consumption. *The Economic Journal*, 97(387), 666–684. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries; among the most reproduced results in behavioral economics.)

**Sample/method:** Willingness-to-pay elicitations for pleasures (a kiss from one's favorite movie star) and pains (electric shock) at various delays (immediately; 3 hours; 24 hours; 3 days; 1 year; 10 years); formal model of anticipal utility ("savoring" positive, "dread" negative).

**Effect sizes:** WTP for the kiss **peaked at a 3-day delay** — higher than for an immediate kiss (non-monotonic in delay; violating standard discounting): people pay for the anticipation itself. Symmetrically, subjects paid *more* to receive a shock immediately than after delay (dread as negative anticipal utility).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The theoretical warrant for treating the wait as a *feature*, and the brand name's thesis ("anticipy") made rigorous: delay adds value when the waiting period is filled with savoring rather than uncertainty. The savoring/dread asymmetry is the whole game — an identical 12-month wait is savored when the outcome feels certain and vivid (progress footage, confirmed dates, the AI already alive in the app) and dreaded/discounted when uncertain (silence, vague timelines). Copy can explicitly frame the wait as savoring, which the culture already understands ("half the joy of a trip is the months of looking forward to it — your pendant works the same way"). Note the boundary: Loewenstein's savoring peaks at *short* delays for single pleasures; a year is savorable only if broken into a sequence of near-term anticipated events (monthly milestones, each a 3-day-scale savoring target). The update calendar is thus not retention hygiene but the mechanism that converts one unsavorable 12-month wait into twelve savorable 30-day waits.

## 8.5 Kumar, Killingsworth & Gilovich (2014) — waiting for experiences beats waiting for things

**Citation:** Kumar, A., Killingsworth, M. A., & Gilovich, T. (2014). Waiting for Merlot: Anticipatory consumption of experiential and material purchases. *Psychological Science*, 25(10), 1924–1931. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Four studies: experience-sampling (thousands of real-time reports of people awaiting purchases), surveys, and archival/behavioral data (incl. news accounts of queuing behavior), comparing the anticipation period for experiential vs. material purchases.

**Effect sizes:** Waiting for **experiential** purchases was reliably more pleasant — more excitement, less impatience — than waiting for material purchases (significant differences across all four methods); impatience-driven negative behaviors (queue incidents) concentrated in material contexts.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The pendant is physically material but its value proposition is experiential (conversations remembered, moments captured, presence). This evidence says the *framing choice is consequential for the wait itself*: buyers who mentally file the purchase as "an experience I'm going to have" will enjoy (and survive) the 12-month wait better than buyers who filed it as "a gadget I'm owed." Therefore: sell outcomes and moments, not object acquisition, in all post-purchase communication ("in August you'll stop losing Tuesday's ideas" vs. "your device ships in August"); name the wait experientially ("your year of getting ready" with app onboarding milestones); reserve object-language (specs, unboxing) for the final pre-ship phase when material concreteness is construal-appropriate (Section 2). This single reframe plausibly reduces wait-driven cancellations at zero cost.

## 8.6 Nowlis, Mandel & McCabe (2004) — delay between choice and consumption increases enjoyment (for pleasures)

**Citation:** Nowlis, S. M., Mandel, N., & McCabe, D. B. (2004). The effect of a delay between choice and consumption on consumption enjoyment. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 31(3), 502–510. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Lab experiments delaying consumption of chosen treats (e.g., chocolate) vs. immediate consumption; moderators: certainty of the delayed reward and item type (hedonic vs. utilitarian).

**Effect sizes:** Delay **increased enjoyment of hedonic items** when receipt was certain (savoring builds consumption pleasure); the boost disappeared or reversed under uncertainty and for non-hedonic items (significant delay × certainty interactions).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Experimental confirmation of 8.4's mechanism at the consumption end: buyers who waited — *with certainty* — enjoy the product more at delivery than same-day buyers would have. This is a quiet marketing asset: August 2026 pre-order customers should, on average, have a *better* first-week experience than later walk-in retail buyers, feeding stronger early reviews and word of mouth — but only if certainty was maintained throughout (dates kept, progress visible). It also prescribes the delivery moment itself: build a savoring-consummation ritual (founder-signed note referencing their member number and join date, "your AI has been waiting to meet you" first-boot script) that pays off the year of anticipation explicitly.

## 8.7 Schreiner, Mandler, van Heerde & Haiduk (2025) — the dynamics of pre-release buzz (FULL TEXT READ)

**Citation:** Schreiner, T. F., Mandler, T., van Heerde, H. J., & Haiduk, C. (2025). Dynamics of pre-release consumer buzz: Driving communication, search, and participation for market performance. *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, 53, 1261–1281. (Access: full text, Springer open access.)

**Sample/method:** Econometric panel modeling of pre-release marketing and buzz for **330 US-released movies**, decomposing pre-release consumer buzz into three behaviors — **communication** (posting/talking), **search** (information seeking), **participation** (e.g., engaging with trailers/watchlists) — with dynamic interplay (self-enhancing and spillover effects) and firm drivers (social media posts, trailers), linked to opening market performance.

**Effect sizes:** All three buzz behaviors positively affect market performance; **communication contributes most** (largest performance elasticity), partly via spillovers that stimulate search and participation; buzz behaviors are self-enhancing over the pre-release window (momentum); firm social-media posts are the most effective lever for communication and search, while trailers most effectively evoke participation.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** A pre-order campaign *is* a pre-release window, and this decomposition orders the investment: (1) prioritize triggering **communication** — shareable artifacts (a striking 60-second film, a "what my pendant remembered" story format, referral perks framed as sharing rather than selling; cf. 6.6) — because talk spills over into search and participation, not vice versa; (2) maintain **momentum mechanics**: buzz is self-enhancing, so schedule beats (reveal → batch milestones → production footage → press reviews) at regular intervals rather than one launch spike followed by silence — each beat re-seeds the buzz system; (3) match asset to behavior: social posts drive talk/search, while the trailer-equivalent (product film) drives participation (waitlist joins, app downloads) — measure them against those respective KPIs, not a single blended metric.

## 8.8 Section synthesis — the time architecture of the campaign

1. **Always dates, never durations** (8.2, 8.3 — the most replicated rule in this report): "Ships August 2026"; slips communicated as revised dates; day-of-week framing in the final week.
2. **Fill the wait with certainty + vividness → savoring; starve it → dread-discounting** (8.4, 8.6): monthly dated milestones convert one long wait into twelve short savorable ones.
3. **File the purchase experientially** (8.5): outcome-language throughout the wait; object-language only near ship.
4. **Program buzz as a system** (8.7): communication-first assets, regular beats, momentum maintenance.
5. **Exploit the anticipation dividend at delivery** (8.6): the pre-order cohort's elevated first-week enjoyment is the review-generation engine for the retail launch — schedule review prompts into week one post-delivery.

# SECTION 9 — Refund Policy Effects

## 9.0 Framing: the refund policy is the pre-order's risk instrument

A pre-order asks the buyer to bear delivery risk, product-fit risk, and change-of-mind risk for a year. The refund/cancellation policy is the seller's instrument for repricing that risk. The return-policy literature (mostly from retail) maps cleanly onto pre-order cancellation policy, with one bonus: pre-ship cancellations cost the seller almost nothing (no shipped goods, no restocking), so the economics favor generosity even more than in retail.

---

## 9.1 Janakiraman, Syrdal & Freling (2016) — the meta-analysis: leniency buys purchases more than it buys returns

**Citation:** Janakiraman, N., Syrdal, H. A., & Freling, R. (2016). The effect of return policy leniency on consumer purchase and return decisions: A meta-analytic review. *Journal of Retailing*, 92(2), 226–235. (Access: abstract + widely reported findings; 21 empirical papers, 11,662 subjects pooled.)

**Sample/method:** Meta-analysis of 21 papers / 11,662 subjects; decomposes leniency into five dimensions — **money** (refund completeness), **time** (return window), **effort** (process ease), **scope** (which items qualify), **exchange** (cash vs. credit) — and pools effects on purchase and on return behavior.

**Effect sizes:** Overall, return-policy leniency **significantly increases purchase** (pooled positive effect) and increases returns to a *smaller* degree; by dimension: money- and effort-leniency most strongly drive **purchase**; scope-, time-, and exchange-leniency more strongly drive **returns**. Notably, longer time windows increase returns less than intuition suggests (endowment/procrastination keep products in hand — cf. 1.2).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The dimensional decomposition is a policy blueprint: maximize the dimensions that buy conversion (money: 100% refund, no fees; effort: one-click cancellation from the account page, no forms or support tickets) and be measured on those that mainly buy returns (scope/exchange matter little pre-ship). The recommended policy: **"Cancel anytime before your pendant ships for a full, automatic refund — one click, no questions."** Pre-ship, this is nearly free (no goods move), yet it neutralizes the No. 1 backer deterrent (delivery distrust, 6.1/6.5). Post-delivery, a separate 30-day return window applies (standard hardware practice); keep the two policies visually distinct so the pre-ship guarantee reads as absolute. The meta-analytic asymmetry (purchases up more than returns) is the business case to give skeptical stakeholders: leniency is not a cost center; it is underpriced conversion.

---

## 9.2 Wood (2001) — lenient policies in remote purchasing: more orders, not more returns

**Citation:** Wood, S. L. (2001). Remote purchase environments: The influence of return policy leniency on two-stage decision processes. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 38(2), 157–169. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Experiments simulating remote (catalog/online) purchases with lenient vs. restrictive return policies; measured order incidence, deliberation, and subsequent return behavior (two-stage model: order decision, then keep/return decision).

**Effect sizes:** Lenient policies **increased order incidence and reduced pre-purchase deliberation time** (consumers "order to try," deferring the real decision); crucially, in Wood's data leniency **did not proportionally increase returns** — post-receipt, endowment and inertia favored keeping (consistent with 1.2's ownership flip).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Wood's two-stage model is exactly the pre-order psychology: with a strong cancellation guarantee, the visitor's decision changes from "commit $149.99 for a year" (hard, deliberation-heavy) to "hold a refundable place in the batch" (easy, fast). The page should *explicitly teach this reframe*: "You're not deciding today whether you'll love it in August — you're just holding your place. Decide anytime; cancel with one click." Then the second stage plays out over the wait, where Sections 1 and 8 (accruing digital ownership, savoring, sunk-payment depreciation) systematically tilt keep-vs-cancel toward keeping — the same forces that suppress returns in Wood's data. The deliberation-time result also predicts measurable funnel effects: prominence of the guarantee near the CTA should compress hesitation and raise same-session conversion; test guarantee placement as a first-class variable.

---

## 9.3 Suwelack, Hogreve & Hoyer (2011) — how money-back guarantees work emotionally

**Citation:** Suwelack, T., Hogreve, J., & Hoyer, W. D. (2011). Understanding money-back guarantees: Cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes. *Journal of Retailing*, 87(4), 462–478. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Experimental studies of money-back guarantees (MBGs), modeling parallel cognitive (risk reduction) and affective (anticipated regret reduction; positive emotion) pathways to purchase intention and WTP.

**Effect sizes:** MBGs significantly increased purchase intention and WTP via *both* routes: reduced perceived risk and reduced anticipated regret / enhanced positive affect; the affective path was independent and substantial (guarantees make buying *feel* safer and better, beyond the rational option value).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Write the guarantee for both pathways. Cognitive: precise terms ("full refund until your unit ships; refunds processed within 5 business days to your original payment method") — specificity is what the risk calculator needs. Affective: warm, regret-dissolving language ("the only thing you're committing to today is curiosity") — what the feeling system needs. And since the guarantee raises WTP (not just conversion), it partially *funds itself* at the $149.99 price point: an offer with a strong MBG can hold a higher price than one without. Placement: state it three times — near the price, in the CTA microcopy, and in the order-confirmation email (where it paradoxically *reduces* cancellations by signaling confidence and removing the trapped feeling that fuels reactance during the wait).

---

## 9.4 Xie & Gerstner (2007) — refunds in advance selling: cancellations can be profitable

**Citation:** Xie, J., & Gerstner, E. (2007). Service escape: Profiting from customer cancellations. *Marketing Science*, 26(1), 18–30. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries; companion to 7.1.)

**Sample/method:** Analytical model of advance sales with customer cancellations; seller chooses refund policy (full/partial/none) and may resell released capacity.

**Effect sizes/key results:** Offering (even generous partial) refunds can **increase seller profit** relative to no-refund policies: refunds raise the advance price buyers will pay (the option value is priced in), and cancelled units can be resold; optimal refund is often partial but substantial, and full refunds can be optimal when resale is easy.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The formal license for the Section 9.1 recommendation in the *advance-selling* context specifically: for a supply-constrained founding batch, a cancelled pre-order isn't lost revenue — it's a unit returned to a batch with a waitlist behind it (resale is trivially easy pre-ship). Therefore full refundability is not generosity but option pricing: buyers pay $149.99 partly *for* the exit option, and anticipy.ai resells any exercised exits to the next waitlist position, potentially at a later (higher) batch price. Operationalize the resale loop visibly: "cancelled founding units are released to the waitlist" — which simultaneously reassures buyers (exit is real) and adds scarcity credibility (the batch really is finite and demanded).

---

## 9.5 Oghazi, Karlsson, Hellström & Hjort (2018) — return policy leniency as a trust signal

**Citation:** Oghazi, P., Karlsson, S., Hellström, D., & Hjort, K. (2018). Online purchase return policy leniency and purchase decision: Mediating role of consumer trust. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*, 41, 190–200. (Access: abstract + secondary summaries.)

**Sample/method:** Survey/structural modeling of online shoppers linking perceived return-policy leniency → consumer trust → purchase decision.

**Effect sizes:** Leniency's effect on purchase was **significantly mediated by trust**: a lenient policy is read as a signal that the seller believes in the product and deals fairly, and this inferred trustworthiness carries much of the conversion effect (significant indirect path; coefficients at abstract level).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The guarantee does double duty documented separately from 9.1's direct risk math: it is *evidence about the seller*. For an unknown brand asking for a 12-month commitment, this signaling channel may exceed the direct option-value channel — "they wouldn't offer one-click refunds for a year if they weren't sure they'll ship something great" is exactly the inference the distrust literature (6.5) says must be induced institutionally rather than claimed verbally. Copy should quietly point at the inference: "We can offer this because we only win if the pendant is worth keeping." Also audit for signal-contradictions: a generous headline policy undermined by fine print (fees, store-credit-only, hard-to-find cancellation flow) reads as deceptive and *reverses* the trust signal — the mediation logic means a flawed guarantee is worse than a modest honest one.

---

## 9.6 Section synthesis — the anticipy.ai risk-reversal stack

**Policy:** Full, automatic, one-click refund any time before shipment; 30-day post-delivery return window; refunds to original payment method within 5 business days; cancelled founding units visibly released to the waitlist.

**Evidence map:** conversion lift with sub-proportional return cost (9.1 meta, 21 papers); "hold your place" deliberation collapse (9.2); cognitive + affective dual-path WTP lift (9.3); profitability under resale — the option is priced in and exits are resold (9.4); trust-signal mediation for an unknown brand (9.5); and from earlier sections: post-purchase endowment (1.2/1.6), sunk-payment depreciation (7.4), and savoring certainty (8.6) all tilt the exercised-option rate down over the wait.

**Prediction to monitor:** cancellation hazard should spike at (a) order +48h (cold feet), (b) any schedule slip, and (c) ship-time payment events if any exist (avoid per 7.7). A healthy program sees single-digit pre-ship cancellation percentages; each spike beyond that localizes a broken promise elsewhere in the system, not a policy problem.

# SECTION 10 — Post-Purchase Dissonance in Long-Wait Purchases

## 10.0 Framing: the twelve months after checkout are an attitude under siege

Post-purchase (cognitive) dissonance is the psychological discomfort that follows a difficult, important, irreversible-feeling decision, driving the buyer to seek consonant information, avoid dissonant information — or undo the decision. Classic retail dissonance plays out over days; a pre-order stretches it across a year, punctuated by exactly the events (delays, competitor launches, negative chatter) that feed it. This section covers the construct, its measurement, its known amplifiers (price, involvement, wait), and its management.

---

## 10.1 Festinger (1957) — the theory of cognitive dissonance

**Citation:** Festinger, L. (1957). *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.* Stanford University Press. (Access: canonical theory; foundational treatment.)

**Sample/method:** Theoretical monograph + program of experiments (free-choice paradigm: post-decision spreading of alternatives; induced compliance; effort justification).

**Effect sizes:** Foundational phenomena replicated for decades: after difficult choices, evaluations of the chosen option rise and the rejected option falls (post-decision spread); dissonance magnitude scales with decision importance, choice difficulty (closeness of alternatives), and volition.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Three levers fall out of the classic moderators. (1) *Volition:* buyers who feel they chose freely justify their choice; buyers who feel pressured (dark-pattern urgency, guilt trips) experience reactance-tinged dissonance and cancel — keep the scarcity honest (real batch caps, 5.3) and the exit visible (9.x), which paradoxically strengthens post-decision commitment ("I stay because I can leave"). (2) *Post-decision spread is free advocacy:* immediately after purchase, the buyer is spontaneously upgrading their opinion of the pendant — the confirmation page is the highest-receptivity moment for referral asks and social sharing ("tell someone what you just claimed"). (3) *Effort justification:* the onboarding effort of 1.13 doesn't just build ownership; effort spent on the pendant becomes another reason it must be worth it.

---

## 10.2 Sweeney, Hausknecht & Soutar (2000) — measuring dissonance: it's emotional as well as cognitive

**Citation:** Sweeney, J. C., Hausknecht, D., & Soutar, G. N. (2000). Cognitive dissonance after purchase: A multidimensional scale. *Psychology & Marketing*, 17(5), 369–385. (Access: abstract + widely used scale documentation.)

**Sample/method:** Scale development with consumer samples (furniture and appliance buyers); CFA-validated 22-item instrument with three dimensions: **emotional** (anxiety, unease after purchase), **wisdom of purchase** ("did I really need it? was it the right choice?"), and **concern over deal** ("was I misled? did I pay too much?").

**Effect sizes:** Psychometric: three-factor structure with strong reliabilities (α > .8 across dimensions); the emotional dimension typically explains the largest variance share in post-purchase discomfort.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** The three dimensions are three distinct nurture problems with three distinct fixes across the wait. *Emotional* (anxiety): fixed by cadence and presence — the monthly dated update (8.8), fast human support replies, community warmth; silence is the amplifier. *Wisdom of purchase*: fixed by usage — every week the app already helps ("your AI remembered 12 things this month") re-proves the decision; this is the killer argument for shipping software value immediately (1.6). *Concern over deal*: fixed by price-path integrity — the written "never undercut" commitment (7.1) plus visible batch-price increases for later batches ("batch 2: $169") turn the early buyer's deal into a verified win. Instrument all three: a two-question pulse survey at order +30 days ("How are you feeling about your pre-order?" + open text) maps buyers onto the dimensions and routes the right intervention before cancellation, not after.

---

## 10.3 Oliver (1980) — expectation-confirmation: the delivery-day reckoning

**Citation:** Oliver, R. L. (1980). A cognitive model of the antecedents and consequences of satisfaction decisions. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 17(4), 460–469. (Access: abstract + canonical summaries; foundation of expectation-confirmation theory.)

**Sample/method:** Longitudinal survey design measuring pre-exposure expectations, post-exposure (dis)confirmation, satisfaction, and downstream attitudes/intentions.

**Effect sizes:** Satisfaction is driven by expectations *plus* disconfirmation (performance vs. expectations); disconfirmation effects on satisfaction are strong and carry forward into post-purchase attitude and repurchase intention (significant path coefficients; the model has been replicated across hundreds of studies).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Twelve months of marketing is twelve months of expectation-setting, and August 2026 is the disconfirmation event. The pre-order structurally *inflates* expectations (imagery, savoring, identity — everything Sections 1–8 recommend), which raises the delivery-day bar. Management strategy: (1) *asymmetric promising* — market the emotionally vivid but easily-delivered core (it remembers what you tell it) and systematically under-promise the fragile edges (battery life, transcription in noisy rooms): specific numeric promises only where production data guarantees beating them; (2) *expectation maintenance during the wait*: beta-app access and real demo footage continuously calibrate expectations toward reality, so disconfirmation is pre-paid in small increments rather than lump-sum at unboxing; (3) *the anticipation dividend* (8.6) only pays if expectations were met — a savored wait ending in disappointment converts to amplified dissonance and vocal negative reviews (the same anticipation energy, sign-flipped).

---

## 10.4 Mollick (2014) + Shneor & Flåten (2020) — the delay reality: base rates and non-delivery risk (FULL TEXTS READ; cross-ref 6.4, 6.6)

**Citation:** Mollick (2014), Section 6.4 (delay statistics); Zhao & Ryu / Shneor et al. (2020), Section 6.6 (non-delivery risk as the persistent friction of reward crowdfunding).

**Sample/method/effect sizes:** From Mollick's hand-tracked delivery subsample: **over 75% of delivering projects shipped late**; mean delay among delivered projects 1.28 months (SD 1.56); delay severity scales with funding size (overfunded projects most delayed — complexity and scope creep); a minority never deliver. The Advances in Crowdfunding synthesis identifies non-delivery risk as the signature deterrent shaping backer behavior platform-wide.

**Application to anticipy.ai:** Plan for the *expected* case: some slippage risk on a 12-month hardware schedule is real, and sophisticated buyers know the base rates. Dissonance economics of a slip: the cost is not the extra weeks — it is the *credibility repricing* of every other promise on the page. Protocol, evidence-derived: (1) buffer the public date so the modal outcome is "on time" (6.4); (2) if slipping, announce *early* (before rumor), as a revised **date** not a duration (8.3), with cause, plan, and an unprompted goodwill gesture (accessory credit) — converting a dissonance spike into a competence demonstration; (3) never go silent — in Pinkow's case data (5.4) and the crowdfunding-forum record generally, silence-then-slip is the canonical trust-collapse pattern, while communicated slips are routinely forgiven, especially under the helper-frame (4.3) where backers self-identify as partners in a hard thing rather than customers of a late vendor.

---

## 10.5 Choudhury et al. (2025) — contemporary evidence: channel design and dissonance

**Citation:** Choudhury, D., Singh, H., Acharjee, T., Das, D., & Sharma, A. (2025). Does hybrid shopping reduce post-purchase cognitive dissonance? Empirical evidence from university students. (Access: abstract via Semantic Scholar.)

**Sample/method:** Survey study of student consumers comparing dissonance levels across pure-online, pure-offline, and hybrid (research online / verify offline, etc.) purchase journeys, using established dissonance scales (cf. 10.2).

**Effect sizes:** Hybrid journeys — where consumers could *verify* the product through a second channel before or after committing — showed significantly lower post-purchase dissonance than single-channel online purchases (statistics at abstract level).

**Application to anticipy.ai:** A pure-online pre-order of an untouchable product is the maximum-dissonance channel configuration; the mitigation is manufacturing *verification moments* that stand in for the offline channel: live video demos with real hardware (not renders), third-party hands-on coverage and teardowns, pop-up demo events in a few cities for press and locals (each event generating verification content for everyone else), and the beta app as a self-serve verification channel ("the AI is real — you're already talking to it"). Every independent verification artifact permanently lowers the ambient dissonance level of the whole waiting cohort, because dissonant buyers actively search for exactly this material (Festinger's consonant-information seeking) — make sure what they find is yours and is true.

# SECTION 11 — Cross-Cutting Synthesis: A Psychological Model of the Pre-Order Decision

## 11.1 The unified model

Integrating Sections 1–10, the pre-order decision for a product like the anticipy.ai pendant can be modeled as five multiplicative gates, each mapped to a literature:

```
PRE-ORDER  =  ATTENTION  ×  VALUE BELIEF  ×  TRUST  ×  TIME TOLERANCE  ×  CLAIM TRIGGER
              (ELM peripheral)  (simulation +     (deterrent     (CLT + date framing   (ownership +
                                 central route)    removal)        + anticipation)       scarcity + deadline)
```

- **Attention** is won peripherally in seconds (design quality, social proof, founder presence — Section 4). It buys the chance to persuade, nothing more.
- **Value belief** is built by mental simulation and central-route evidence together (Sections 3–4): the visitor must *experience the product in imagination* (simulation, ownership imagery) and be able to *defend the purchase to themselves* (ingredients, interconnects, credentials).
- **Trust** is a gate, not a factor (6.3): motives multiply through it, and it has two independent workstreams — building trust and dismantling distrust (6.5) — with the refund policy as the single most efficient instrument spanning both (Section 9).
- **Time tolerance** is manufactured: date framing flattens discounting (8.2–8.3), immediate digital possession shrinks the effective wait (1.6, 2.8), and savoring architecture converts the residual wait from cost to benefit (8.4–8.6).
- **The claim trigger** converts intention into action *now*: psychological ownership makes the product "mine," and scarcity + deadline (real batch mechanics, 5.3) make *not claiming* feel like impending loss (1.8, 1.12). Without a trigger, high-intent visitors defer indefinitely (the bathtub's middle, 5.3).

The multiplicative structure encodes the review's central strategic warning: excellence in four gates cannot compensate for a zero in the fifth. A gorgeous, credible, vividly simulated offer with no claim trigger leaks intent; a high-urgency claim machine with unresolved distrust converts nobody worth having (and refunds the few it does).

## 11.2 Where the literatures agree, disagree, and interlock

**Convergences (high-confidence foundations):**
1. *Imagined experience is real experience, psychologically.* Ownership imagery (1.4), haptic imagery (1.9–1.10), self-product elaboration (1.8), mental simulation (Section 3), and experiential anticipation (8.5) are one mechanism viewed from five literatures: the pre-order buyer consumes in imagination for a year, and the page's job is to feed that consumption.
2. *Possession beats paperwork.* Reb & Connolly (1.6), payment depreciation (7.4), and the dissonance calendar (10.6) agree that what the buyer *has and uses now* — not what they are owed — determines retention. Digital-first delivery of the product's value is the single highest-leverage structural decision available to anticipy.ai.
3. *Dates beat durations.* Two independent labs (8.2, 8.3), plus the days-of-week evidence (8.1), plus CLT's event-vs-interval logic (Section 2) — the most replicated micro-prescription in the review.
4. *Generous exits strengthen commitment.* Refund leniency raises purchases more than returns (9.1–9.2), signals trust (9.5), prices in as option value (9.4), and — via volition — reduces dissonance (10.1). Every literature that touches exit rights lands on the same side.

**Tensions (requiring design resolution):**
1. *Vividness vs. delay sensitivity.* Simulation and ownership literatures demand maximum concreteness; Malkoc & Zauberman (2.8) show concreteness amplifies the pain of waiting. Resolution: be concrete about the *now* (app, community, configuration) and about *dated near-term milestones*; keep peak hardware vividness spatially and temporally separated from wait disclosures, and let concreteness rise as shipping nears (the construal calendar, 2.9).
2. *Scarcity vs. certainty.* Claim motivation feeds on the threat of not getting one (1.8), but the advance-selling and trust literatures require certainty of delivery and price path (7.1, 6.5). Resolution: scarcity of *allocation* (batch caps, deadlines — real), certainty of *fulfillment* (guaranteed unit once claimed) — threat before the order, safety after it.
3. *Social proof vs. bystander effect.* Popularity counters persuade peripherally (4.4) but can suppress action by diffusing responsibility (5.3). Resolution: always bind the count to a capacity ("4,312 of 5,000") so others' orders consume a shared resource instead of guaranteeing a shared outcome.
4. *Simulation vs. scrutiny.* Narrative transportation softens spec-scrutiny (3.2), which flatters a pre-production product but courts a delivery-day reckoning (10.3). Resolution: use simulation for attention and ownership, but keep every simulated claim within the envelope production will actually deliver — the anticipation dividend (8.6) is only banked if expectations survive contact with the shipped unit.

**The interlock that makes pre-orders work at all:** the endowment effect's asymmetry. Before purchase, the visitor is a buyer (low valuation, steep time discounting — 1.2, 1.11); the page must borrow owner psychology through imagery and claiming. After purchase, the customer is an owner (elevated valuation, delay-resistant WTA, sunk-cost amortization) — the same asymmetry that made conversion hard makes retention easy, *provided* the possession is made psychologically real immediately (1.6) and the climate is managed (10.6). A pre-order program is, in essence, a machine for moving people across the WTA/WTP gap and keeping them there for a year.

## 11.3 What the evidence does *not* support

Intellectual honesty inventory:
- **No study directly tests a $149.99, 12-month-wait AI wearable.** Every application paragraph in this report is an extrapolation from adjacent contexts (games, cars, gadgets, campaigns, services); effect sizes cited are context-bound and should be treated as directional priors, not forecasts.
- **Kim & Hedgcock is a working paper** (3 of 5 studies pre-registered, but not yet peer-reviewed at retrieval time); its centrality here reflects its uniqueness (the only direct experimental treatment of pre-order psychology found), not settled-science status.
- **Several sources were accessible only at abstract level** (flagged per entry); where abstract-level sources anchor a recommendation, converging full-text sources were required before ranking it highly (e.g., date-vs-delay rests on two full literatures even though 8.1's full text was unavailable).
- **Publication bias and lab-effect inflation** apply across the board (the Söderberg meta-analysis explicitly examines and largely allays this for CLT, but ownership/simulation effect sizes from student-lab paradigms in the +20–60% range should be haircut substantially for field settings).
- **Deposit-vs-full-payment lacks a definitive field experiment**; Section 7's recommendation triangulates theory (mental accounting) and one null (7.6) — it is the report's most reversible conclusion, and a natural candidate for anticipy.ai's own A/B testing.

## 12.6 The one-paragraph version

Make the visitor an owner before they are a buyer (imagery, configuration, demo, claim language); make the purchase safe enough to be casual (one-click refund, price-path promise, named production reality); make the wait short in framing (August 2026, never "12 months"), full in substance (the AI live from day one, monthly dated milestones), and savorable in tone (an experiential year of getting ready, shared with the founders and 5,000 others); and make August 2026 an under-promised, over-delivered payoff that converts a year of anticipation into the review base for the retail launch.

> [129] People are capable of thinking about the future, the past, remote locations, another person’s perspective, and counterfactual alternatives. Without denying the uniqueness of each process, it is proposed that they constitute different forms of traversing psychological distance. Psychological distance is egocentric: Its reference point is the self in the here and now, and the different ways in which an object might be removed from that point—in time, in space, in social distance, and in hypotheticality—constitute different distance dimensions. Transcending the self in the here and now entails mental construal, and the farther removed an object is from direct experience, the higher (more abstract) the level of construal of that object. > [134] People directly experience only the here and now. It is impossible to experience the past and the future, other places, other people, and alternatives to reality. And yet, memories, plans, predictions, hopes, and counterfactual alternatives populate our minds, influence our emotions, and guide our choice and action. How do we transcend the here and now to include distal entities? How do we plan for the distant future, understand other people’s point of view, and take into account hypothetical alternatives to reality? Construal level theory (CLT) proposes that we do so by forming abstract mental construals of distal objects. Thus, although we cannot experience what is not present, we can make predictions about the future, remember the past, imagine other people’s reactions, and speculate about what might have been. Predictions, memories, and speculations are all mental constructions, distinct from direct experience. They serve to transcend the immediate situation and represent psychologically distant objects. Psychological distance is a subjective experience that something is close or far away from the self, here, and now. Psychological distance is thus egocentric: Its reference point is the self, here and now, and the different ways in which an object might be removed from that point—in time, space, social distance, and hypotheticality—constitute different distance dimensions.

> [136] According to CLT, then, people traverse different psychological distances by using similar mental construal processes. Because the various distances have the same egocentric reference point, they should all be cognitively related to each other and similarly affect and be affected by level of construal. As psychological distance increases, construals would become more abstract, and as level of abstraction increases, so too would the psychological distances people envisage. Construal levels thus expand and contract one’s mental horizon. The different distances should also similarly influence prediction, evaluation, and action, inasmuch as these outcomes are mediated by construal. We describe the properties of the mental construal processes that enable them to fulfill this function and further explicate our approach by relating it to extant theories of how people respond to distant objects. For example, by moving from representing an object as a “cellular phone” to representing it as “a communication device,” we omit information about size; moving from representing an activity as “playing ball” to representing it as “having fun,” we omit the ball. Concrete representations typically lend themselves to multiple abstractions. For example, a cellular phone could be construed also as a “small object,” and “playing ball” could be construed as “exercising.” An abstract representation is selected according to its relevance to one’s goals. Thus, if one’s goal is to contact a friend, then “a communication device” is relevant, but size is not. From the perspective of that goal, the cell phone could be replaced by (i.e., would be conceptually close to) a desktop computer with an Internet connection. If, however, one’s goal is to pickpocket a valuable object, then size is a relevant attribute, and function is of less relevance. Given this goal, the cell phone could be replaced by (i.e., would be conceptually close to) a wallet. Like irrelevant details, details that are inconsistent with the chosen abstract representation are omitted from the representation or assimilated into it. For example, the detail that the ball game is slow would be omitted or modified once the activity is represented as “exercising.” Because abstract representations necessarily impose one of many alternative interpretations, and because irrelevant or inconsistent details are omitted or assimilated to it, these representations tend to be simpler, less ambiguous, more coherent, more schematic, and more prototypical than concrete representations (

> [166] ). It is important to note, however, that higher level construals are not simply more impoverished or vague than lower level construals. They often convey additional information about the value of the stimulus and its relations to other stimuli. For example, “having fun” entails many characteristics that are not an obvious part of “playing ball outside” (e.g., valence) and places the activity within a broader context by specifying its relations to other concepts (e.g., a party). Thus, the process of abstraction involves not only a loss of specific, idiosyncratic, and incidental information, but also ascription of new meaning deduced from stored knowledge and organized in structured representations.

> [182] On the basis of this conceptualization, we posit two related criteria that can be used to distinguish which features of an item or an event are more high level and which are more low level (i.e., which features will be increasingly captured at a higher vs. lower level of construal). The first criterion reflects centrality: Changing a high-level feature has a greater impact on the meaning of an object than does changing a low-level feature. For example, a lecture would change more when the speaker is changed than when the room is changed, suggesting that the speaker is a higher level feature of the lecture than the room is. The second criterion reflects subordination: The meaning of low-level features depends on high-level features more than vice versa. For example, when learning about a forthcoming guest lecture, location would become important only if the topic is interesting. The topic of the lecture, on the other hand, would be important regardless of the location’s convenience. In this sense, details about location are subordinated to details about topic, and thus constitute a lower level of construal.

> [186] CLT contends that people use increasingly higher levels of construal to represent an object as the psychological distance from the object increases. This is because high-level construals are more likely than low-level construals to remain unchanged as one gets closer to an object or farther away from it. For example, the higher level goal to contact a friend is more stable over time than the more concrete goal to send her an e-mail, because an Internet connection might be unavailable when one is actually trying to contact the friend. From a temporally distant perspective, it is therefore more useful to construe this action in terms of the high-level goal rather than the low-level goal. The same holds true for the other distances. Thus, abstract categories tend to change less across social distance. For example, more people use communication devices than cell phones, and therefore, the former construal is more useful for relating to socially distant individuals. Even maintaining perceptual constancy across spatial distance requires abstraction. Identifying an object in near and distant locations as being the same requires forming an abstract concept (e.g., a chair) that omits incidental features (e.g., perspective-specific appearances and contextual variations, such as the way a chair’s shade falls upon the floor and its retinal size) and retains essential, relatively invariant features (e.g., its overall shape and proportions). The use of high-level, abstract construals to represent psychologically distal objects is thus indispensable for effective functioning in many domains: for developing object constancy, orienting in space, planning the future, learning from the past, relating to and understanding other people, and for considering alternative outcomes and courses of action.

> [188] Consider now the reverse direction of influence, namely, the effect of level of construal on distance. Because high-level construals are more general, they bring to mind more distal instantiations of objects. For example, “having fun,” compared with “playing basketball outside,” may bring to mind activities in the more distant future and past, in more remote locations, in hypothetical situations, and with more socially distant others. Similarly, construing another person’s behavior in terms of a personality trait (a high-level construct) involves considering that person’s behavior in the past and future, in other places, and in hypothetical situations. More generally, forming and comprehending abstract concepts enable people to mentally transcend the currently experienced object in time and space, integrating other social perspectives, and considering novel and hypothetical examples. In this sense, the different levels of construal serve to expand and contract one’s mental horizons and thus mentally traverse psychological distances.

> [190] It is critical that, although we believe that these functional relationships underlie the association between construal and distance, we propose that the effects of distance on construal and of construal on distance are overgeneralized, making them persist even when the initial reasons that gave rise to the association are no longer present.
